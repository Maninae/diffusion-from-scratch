11.1 The Prediction Target Problem

Throughout this course, we've trained our diffusion model to predict the noise $\varepsilon$ that was added to a clean image. This is the DDPM default, and it works well. But it has a flaw that becomes visible when you look closely at training dynamics across timesteps.

Recall the forward process: $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} \cdot x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \cdot \varepsilon$. At different timesteps, the balance between signal and noise changes dramatically:

  • At low noise ($t$ near 0): $x_t \approx x_0$ with a tiny sprinkle of noise. Predicting that tiny noise accurately is hard; the loss signal is weak, and gradients are small.
  • At high noise ($t$ near $T$): $x_t \approx \varepsilon$ with almost no signal. Predicting noise is numerically stable here, but the model has almost nothing to work with, so it's essentially guessing.

Can we do better? Let's look at what happens when we change what the model predicts.

11.2 Three Prediction Parameterizations

With that uneven behavior of $\varepsilon$-prediction in mind, let's look at the alternatives. All three approaches below are mathematically equivalent; you can convert freely between them given $(x_t, t)$. They differ in numerical stability and gradient behavior across timesteps.

ε-Prediction (DDPM Standard)

The model predicts the noise $\varepsilon$ that was added to $x_0$:

$$\mathcal{L}_\varepsilon = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, \varepsilon}\Big[\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\|^2\Big]$$
  • Strengths: stable at high noise ($t$ near $T$), well-studied, the default choice for most tutorials and papers
  • Weakness: at low noise ($t$ near 0), the noise $\varepsilon$ is scaled down by $\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$ which approaches zero, so the loss signal per training step is tiny

x0-Prediction

The model predicts the clean image $x_0$ directly:

$$\mathcal{L}_{x_0} = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, \varepsilon}\Big[\|x_0 - \hat{x}_0(x_t, t)\|^2\Big]$$
  • Strengths: intuitive ("what did the image look like before we added noise?"). Good at low noise timesteps where $x_t \approx x_0$.
  • Weakness: at high noise, predicting $x_0$ from near-pure noise produces large errors and unstable gradients. The model is asked to hallucinate a clean image from almost nothing.

v-Prediction (Salimans & Ho, 2022)

Define the velocity:

$$v = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} \cdot \varepsilon - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \cdot x_0$$

The model predicts this velocity $v$, and the loss is:

$$\mathcal{L}_v = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, \varepsilon}\Big[\|v - v_\theta(x_t, t)\|^2\Big]$$

Why "velocity"? Think of the forward process as moving along a path from $x_0$ to $\varepsilon$ in a space parameterized by the noise level. The velocity $v$ is the direction and magnitude of that motion. At each timestep, $v$ is a blend of noise and signal, weighted so that:

  • At high noise ($\bar\alpha_t$ small): $v \approx -x_0$, so predicting $v$ is roughly predicting the (negated) clean image—which is stable because the signal component is small in $x_t$
  • At low noise ($\bar\alpha_t$ close to 1): $v \approx \varepsilon$, so predicting $v$ is roughly predicting the noise—but now the noise is the dominant component in $x_t$

In other words, $v$-prediction automatically adapts to whatever's easier at each timestep. It's numerically stable across the entire range, with balanced gradients everywhere.

Key Insight
$v$-prediction interpolates between "predict noise" (at high $t$) and "predict image" (at low $t$), adapting to whichever is more tractable. This is why it's the default for production systems like Imagen and SD 2.x.

11.3 Conversion Between Parameterizations

Since all three parameterizations are mathematically equivalent, you can convert between any two given $(x_t, t)$. The key identity you always start from is the forward process:

$$x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} \cdot x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \cdot \varepsilon$$

Here's the complete conversion table. Let $\alpha = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ and $\sigma = \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$ for readability:

Prediction Target Conversions

From $\varepsilon$ to other targets:

  • $x_0 = (x_t - \sigma \cdot \varepsilon) \;/\; \alpha$
  • $v = \alpha \cdot \varepsilon - \sigma \cdot x_0$

From $x_0$ to other targets:

  • $\varepsilon = (x_t - \alpha \cdot x_0) \;/\; \sigma$
  • $v = \alpha \cdot \varepsilon - \sigma \cdot x_0$

From $v$ to other targets:

  • $x_0 = \alpha \cdot x_t - \sigma \cdot v$
  • $\varepsilon = \sigma \cdot x_t + \alpha \cdot v$

where $\alpha = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ and $\sigma = \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$

Let's verify one of these. From $v$-prediction, recovering $x_0$:

$$x_0 = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} \cdot x_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \cdot v$$

Substitute $v = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} \cdot \varepsilon - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \cdot x_0$ and $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} \cdot x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \cdot \varepsilon$:

$$\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} \cdot x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \cdot \varepsilon) - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} \cdot \varepsilon - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \cdot x_0)$$ $$= \bar\alpha_t \cdot x_0 + \underbrace{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t)} \cdot \varepsilon - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t)} \cdot \varepsilon}_{= \; 0} + (1-\bar\alpha_t) \cdot x_0 = x_0 \;\; \checkmark$$

Conversion in Code

# Conversion utilities # alpha = sqrt(alpha_bar_t), sigma = sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t) def eps_to_x0(x_t, eps, alpha, sigma): return (x_t - sigma * eps) / alpha def eps_to_v(eps, x0, alpha, sigma): return alpha * eps - sigma * x0 def v_to_x0(x_t, v, alpha, sigma): return alpha * x_t - sigma * v def v_to_eps(x_t, v, alpha, sigma): return sigma * x_t + alpha * v

11.4 When to Use Which Parameterization

Since any parameterization can be converted to any other, the choice comes down to training dynamics and practical considerations.

  • $\varepsilon$-prediction: the safe default. Use when following DDPM tutorials, starting a new project, or when simplicity matters more than peak performance. This is what we've used throughout the course.
  • $x_0$-prediction: useful when you want to inspect the model's "current best guess" at $x_0$ during sampling. This is handy for dynamic thresholding (clipping predicted $x_0$ to a valid range). Sometimes better with cosine schedules.
  • $v$-prediction: the modern production default. Use when training with schedules that approach zero terminal SNR (see below), when you need stability across all timesteps, or when doing few-step sampling with DDIM. Used in Imagen, SD 2.x, and most recent systems.
Practical Note
The choice of prediction target matters less than getting the rest of the pipeline right. Any of the three parameterizations will produce good results with proper training. If you're agonizing over $\varepsilon$ vs. $v$, your time is better spent on data quality, schedule tuning, and training stability.

11.5 Advanced Noise Schedules

In Lecture 2, we introduced linear and cosine noise schedules. These get you most of the way there, but modern production systems use several refinements that fix subtle issues. Let's look at each one.

Cosine Schedule (Recap)

Nichol & Dhariwal (2021) introduced the cosine schedule where $\bar\alpha_t$ follows a cosine curve instead of a linear decay. The information in $x_t$ is preserved longer, giving the model more signal to work with at medium timesteps. This is generally preferred over linear for all applications; there's rarely a reason to use a linear schedule.

Offset Noise

There's a subtle but important problem with standard noise. Standard Gaussian noise $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ has zero mean per channel. Every noise sample's red, green, and blue channels each average to approximately zero. This means:

  • During training, $x_t$ always has roughly the same mean brightness as $x_0$
  • The model learns that "average brightness doesn't change much"
  • At inference, starting from $z_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ (zero mean), the model can't generate images that are entirely dark or entirely bright

You might notice this as a bias toward medium-brightness outputs, or an inability to produce dramatic lighting.

The fix (Lin et al., 2023) is just two lines of code:

# Standard noise (zero mean per channel) noise = torch.randn_like(x) # Offset noise: add a per-channel mean shift noise = torch.randn_like(x) + 0.1 * torch.randn(B, C, 1, 1) # ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^ # This shifts the channel mean, # allowing dark/bright generation

The randn(B, C, 1, 1) term is a single random value per channel per sample, broadcast across all spatial positions. It shifts the entire channel up or down, teaching the model that brightness can change. The $0.1$ scaling is a hyperparameter: too large and you distort the diffusion process, too small and the effect is negligible.

Zero Terminal SNR

The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) at timestep $t$ is:

$$\text{SNR}(t) = \frac{\bar\alpha_t}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$$

Here's the problem: many noise schedules don't actually reach $\text{SNR} = 0$ at $t = T$. There's a tiny bit of residual signal ($\bar\alpha_T$ is very small but not exactly zero). This creates a train-test mismatch:

  • Training: at $t = T$, the model sees $x_T$ with a faint trace of signal
  • Inference: we start from pure $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$—no signal at all

The model was never trained on the exact distribution it encounters at the start of sampling. This mismatch can cause subtle artifacts, especially in color distribution and global structure.

The fix (Lin et al., 2023): enforce $\bar\alpha_T = 0$ exactly, so $\text{SNR}(T) = 0$. Now the last timestep during training uses pure noise, matching the inference starting point perfectly.

Definition
Zero terminal SNR means enforcing $\bar\alpha_T = 0$ so that $x_T$ at the final timestep is pure noise $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$, exactly matching the inference starting distribution. This eliminates the train-test distribution gap.

There's a catch: when $\bar\alpha_T = 0$, $\varepsilon$-prediction becomes undefined at $t = T$ because the conversion $x_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_T} \cdot \varepsilon) / \sqrt{\bar\alpha_T}$ requires dividing by zero. This is one of the key reasons $v$-prediction pairs naturally with zero terminal SNR—the $v$ formulation remains well-defined across the entire range.

Continuous-Time Schedules

All our schedules so far use discrete timesteps $t \in \{0, 1, \ldots, T-1\}$. But we can also define schedules over continuous time $t \in [0, 1]$, where $\bar\alpha(t)$ is a smooth function:

  • Training: sample $t \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 1)$ uniformly
  • Inference: choose any set of timesteps $t_1 > t_2 > \cdots > t_N$ with any $N$

The big advantage: you can use arbitrary step counts at inference without modifying the schedule. Train once, sample with 10 steps or 1000 steps. This is the natural formulation for flow matching (Lecture 13), where the continuous-time perspective simplifies everything.

Log-SNR Linear Schedule

A schedule that's linear in log-SNR space rather than in $\beta$ or $\bar\alpha$ space. Since $\log \text{SNR}(t)$ decreases linearly, each timestep represents an equal "difficulty" step for the model. This produces a more uniform training signal—no timesteps are much harder or easier than others.

11.6 Putting It All Together

A decision table for choosing prediction targets and schedules based on the use case:

Practical Configuration Guide

Just getting started:

  • Prediction: $\varepsilon$
  • Schedule: linear or cosine
  • Why: simplest, most documented, easiest to debug

Production model (quality matters):

  • Prediction: $v$
  • Schedule: cosine + zero terminal SNR
  • Why: stable gradients, no train-test mismatch

Dark/bright image generation:

  • Prediction: $v$ + offset noise
  • Schedule: cosine + zero terminal SNR
  • Why: offset noise fixes the brightness bias

Few-step sampling (speed matters):

  • Prediction: $v$
  • Schedule: cosine + zero terminal SNR
  • Why: $v$-prediction + DDIM produces the best results with fewer steps

11.7 Training with v-Prediction

Switching from $\varepsilon$-prediction to $v$-prediction requires very few code changes. Here's the diff:

# ε-prediction training step (what we've been doing) noise = torch.randn_like(x_0) x_t = sqrt_alpha_bar[t] * x_0 + sqrt_one_minus_alpha_bar[t] * noise predicted_noise = model(x_t, t) loss = F.mse_loss(predicted_noise, noise) # v-prediction training step (what changes) noise = torch.randn_like(x_0) x_t = sqrt_alpha_bar[t] * x_0 + sqrt_one_minus_alpha_bar[t] * noise v_target = sqrt_alpha_bar[t] * noise - sqrt_one_minus_alpha_bar[t] * x_0 # NEW predicted_v = model(x_t, t) # same model loss = F.mse_loss(predicted_v, v_target) # NEW target

Only three lines change. The model architecture is identical; it still takes $(x_t, t)$ and outputs a tensor of the same shape. Only the target and the loss computation change. The sampling loop needs corresponding updates to convert the predicted $v$ back to $\varepsilon$ or $x_0$ at each step, using the formulas from the conversion table above.
