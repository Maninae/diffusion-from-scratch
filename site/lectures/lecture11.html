Prediction Targets & Advanced Schedules

Three ways to parameterize what a diffusion model predicts, conversion formulas between them, and the noise schedule improvements that make modern systems production-ready.

We'll compare three prediction targets (ε\varepsilonε, x0x_0x0​, and vvv), see why each handles different timesteps differently, and then turn to the noise schedule improvements — cosine schedules, zero terminal SNR, offset noise — that fixed real production problems in systems like Imagen and Stable Diffusion.

11.1 The Prediction Target Problem

Throughout this course, we've trained our diffusion model to predict the noise ε\varepsilonε that was added to a clean image. This is the DDPM default, and it works well. But it has a flaw that becomes visible when you look closely at training dynamics across timesteps.

Recall the forward process: xt=αˉt⋅x0+1−αˉt⋅εx_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} \cdot x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \cdot \varepsilonxt​=αˉt​​⋅x0​+1−αˉt​​⋅ε. At different timesteps, the balance between signal and noise changes dramatically:

  • At low noise (ttt near 0): xt≈x0x_t \approx x_0xt​≈x0​ with a tiny sprinkle of noise. Predicting that tiny noise accurately is hard; the loss signal is weak, and gradients are small.
  • At high noise (ttt near TTT): xt≈εx_t \approx \varepsilonxt​≈ε with almost no signal. Predicting noise is numerically stable here, but the model has almost nothing to work with, so it's essentially guessing.

Can we do better? Let's look at what happens when we change what the model predicts.

11.2 Three Prediction Parameterizations

With that uneven behavior of ε\varepsilonε-prediction in mind, let's look at the alternatives. All three approaches below are mathematically equivalent; you can convert freely between them given (xt,t)(x_t, t)(xt​,t). They differ in numerical stability and gradient behavior across timesteps.

ε-Prediction (DDPM Standard)

The model predicts the noise ε\varepsilonε that was added to x0x_0x0​:

Lε=Et,x0,ε[∥ε−εθ(xt,t)∥2]\mathcal{L}_\varepsilon = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, \varepsilon}\Big[\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\|^2\Big]Lε​=Et,x0​,ε​[∥ε−εθ​(xt​,t)∥2]
  • Strengths: stable at high noise (ttt near TTT), well-studied, the default choice for most tutorials and papers
  • Weakness: at low noise (ttt near 0), the noise ε\varepsilonε is scaled down by 1−αˉt\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}1−αˉt​​ which approaches zero, so the loss signal per training step is tiny

x0-Prediction

The model predicts the clean image x0x_0x0​ directly:

Lx0=Et,x0,ε[∥x0−x^0(xt,t)∥2]\mathcal{L}_{x_0} = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, \varepsilon}\Big[\|x_0 - \hat{x}_0(x_t, t)\|^2\Big]Lx0​​=Et,x0​,ε​[∥x0​−x^0​(xt​,t)∥2]
  • Strengths: intuitive ("what did the image look like before we added noise?"). Good at low noise timesteps where xt≈x0x_t \approx x_0xt​≈x0​.
  • Weakness: at high noise, predicting x0x_0x0​ from near-pure noise produces large errors and unstable gradients. The model is asked to hallucinate a clean image from almost nothing.

v-Prediction (Salimans & Ho, 2022)

Define the velocity:

v=αˉt⋅ε−1−αˉt⋅x0v = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} \cdot \varepsilon - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \cdot x_0v=αˉt​​⋅ε−1−αˉt​​⋅x0​

The model predicts this velocity vvv, and the loss is:

Lv=Et,x0,ε[∥v−vθ(xt,t)∥2]\mathcal{L}_v = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, \varepsilon}\Big[\|v - v_\theta(x_t, t)\|^2\Big]Lv​=Et,x0​,ε​[∥v−vθ​(xt​,t)∥2]

Why "velocity"? Think of the forward process as moving along a path from x0x_0x0​ to ε\varepsilonε in a space parameterized by the noise level. The velocity vvv is the direction and magnitude of that motion. At each timestep, vvv is a blend of noise and signal, weighted so that:

  • At high noise (αˉt\bar\alpha_tαˉt​ small): v≈−x0v \approx -x_0v≈−x0​, so predicting vvv is roughly predicting the (negated) clean image—which is stable because the signal component is small in xtx_txt​
  • At low noise (αˉt\bar\alpha_tαˉt​ close to 1): v≈εv \approx \varepsilonv≈ε, so predicting vvv is roughly predicting the noise—but now the noise is the dominant component in xtx_txt​

In other words, vvv-prediction automatically adapts to whatever's easier at each timestep. It's numerically stable across the entire range, with balanced gradients everywhere.

Key Insight
vvv-prediction interpolates between "predict noise" (at high ttt) and "predict image" (at low ttt), adapting to whichever is more tractable. This is why it's the default for production systems like Imagen and SD 2.x.

11.3 Training with v-Prediction

Switching from ε\varepsilonε-prediction to vvv-prediction requires very few code changes. Here's the diff:

# ε-prediction training step (what we've been doing) noise = torch.randn_like(x_0) x_t = sqrt_alpha_bar[t] * x_0 + sqrt_one_minus_alpha_bar[t] * noise predicted_noise = model(x_t, t) loss = F.mse_loss(predicted_noise, noise) # v-prediction training step (what changes) noise = torch.randn_like(x_0) x_t = sqrt_alpha_bar[t] * x_0 + sqrt_one_minus_alpha_bar[t] * noise v_target = sqrt_alpha_bar[t] * noise - sqrt_one_minus_alpha_bar[t] * x_0 # NEW predicted_v = model(x_t, t) # same model loss = F.mse_loss(predicted_v, v_target) # NEW target

Only three lines change. The model architecture is identical; it still takes (xt,t)(x_t, t)(xt​,t) and outputs a tensor of the same shape. Only the target and the loss computation change. The sampling loop needs corresponding updates to convert the predicted vvv back to ε\varepsilonε or x0x_0x0​ at each step, using the formulas from the conversion table above.

11.4 Conversion Between Parameterizations

Since all three parameterizations are mathematically equivalent, you can convert between any two given (xt,t)(x_t, t)(xt​,t). The key identity you always start from is the forward process:

xt=αˉt⋅x0+1−αˉt⋅εx_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} \cdot x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \cdot \varepsilonxt​=αˉt​​⋅x0​+1−αˉt​​⋅ε

Conversion in Code

# Conversion utilities # alpha = sqrt(alpha_bar_t), sigma = sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t) def eps_to_x0(x_t, eps, alpha, sigma): return (x_t - sigma * eps) / alpha def eps_to_v(eps, x0, alpha, sigma): return alpha * eps - sigma * x0 def v_to_x0(x_t, v, alpha, sigma): return alpha * x_t - sigma * v def v_to_eps(x_t, v, alpha, sigma): return sigma * x_t + alpha * v

11.5 When to Use Which Parameterization

Since any parameterization can be converted to any other, the choice comes down to training dynamics and practical considerations.

  • ε\varepsilonε-prediction: the safe default. Use when following DDPM tutorials, starting a new project, or when simplicity matters more than peak performance. This is what we've used throughout the course.
  • x0x_0x0​-prediction: useful when you want to inspect the model's "current best guess" at x0x_0x0​ during sampling. This is handy for dynamic thresholding (clipping predicted x0x_0x0​ to a valid range). Sometimes better with cosine schedules.
  • vvv-prediction: a newer formulation. Worth experimenting when training with schedules that approach zero terminal SNR (see below), when you need stability across all timesteps, or when doing few-step sampling with DDIM. Used in Imagen and SD 2.x.
Practical Note
The choice of prediction target matters less than getting the rest of the pipeline right. Any of the three parameterizations will produce good results with proper training. If you're agonizing over ε\varepsilonε vs. vvv, your time is better spent on data quality, schedule tuning, and training stability. In Lecture 13, we'll see a even more generalized form of diffusion modeling called flow matching used in state-of-the-art work.

11.6 Advanced Noise Optimizations & Schedules

In Lecture 2, we introduced linear and cosine noise schedules. These get you most of the way there, but modern production systems use several refinements that fix subtle issues. Let's look at each one.

Cosine Schedule (Recap)

Nichol & Dhariwal (2021) introduced the cosine schedule where αˉt\bar\alpha_tαˉt​ follows a cosine curve instead of a linear decay. The information in xtx_txt​ is preserved longer, giving the model more signal to work with at medium timesteps. The cosine schedule became the standard for good reasons:

  • Linear schedules destroy information too quickly at early timesteps. The signal-to-noise ratio drops steeply early on, meaning fine details like textures and edges are buried in noise before the model gets a chance to learn from them.
  • The cosine curve keeps ᾱ_t high for longer, giving the model more training signal at medium noise levels, which is where the most useful learning happens.
  • Linear schedules waste capacity at the extremes. At very low noise, the denoising task is trivially easy; at very high noise, it is nearly impossible. The linear schedule spends too many timesteps in these uninformative regimes.
  • The cosine schedule produces better sample quality across datasets and architectures, which is why it became the default in Improved DDPM and most subsequent work.

Linear vs. cosine noise schedules. The cosine schedule preserves signal longer through the middle timesteps, giving the model more useful training gradients.

Offset Noise

There's a subtle but important problem with standard noise. Standard Gaussian noise ε∼N(0,I)\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)ε∼N(0,I) has zero mean per channel. Every noise sample's red, green, and blue channels each average to approximately zero. This means:

  • During training, xtx_txt​ always has roughly the same mean brightness as x0x_0x0​
  • The model learns that "average brightness doesn't change much"
  • At inference, starting from zT∼N(0,I)z_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)zT​∼N(0,I) (zero mean), the model can't generate images that are entirely dark or entirely bright

You might notice this as a bias toward medium-brightness outputs, or an inability to produce dramatic lighting.

The fix (Lin et al., 2023) is just two lines of code:

# Standard noise (zero mean per channel) noise = torch.randn_like(x) # Offset noise: add a per-channel mean shift noise = torch.randn_like(x) + 0.1 * torch.randn(B, C, 1, 1) # ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^ # This shifts the channel mean, # allowing dark/bright generation

The randn(B, C, 1, 1) term is a single random value per channel per sample, broadcast across all spatial positions. It shifts the entire channel up or down, teaching the model that brightness can change. The 0.10.10.1 scaling is a hyperparameter: too large and you distort the diffusion process, too small and the effect is negligible.

Without offset noise

With offset noise

Without offset noise, the model can't produce truly dark or bright images: a "dark alley" has ultra-bright highlights, and snowy slopes consistently include darker regions to compensate (trees, shadows). With offset noise, the same prompts produce the full brightness range. Images from CrossLabs.

Zero Terminal SNR

The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) at timestep ttt is:

SNR(t)=αˉt1−αˉt\text{SNR}(t) = \frac{\bar\alpha_t}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}SNR(t)=1−αˉt​αˉt​​

Here's the problem: many noise schedules don't actually reach SNR=0\text{SNR} = 0SNR=0 at t=Tt = Tt=T. There's a tiny bit of residual signal (αˉT\bar\alpha_TαˉT​ is very small but not exactly zero). This creates a train-test mismatch:

  • Training: at t=Tt = Tt=T, the model sees xTx_TxT​ with a faint trace of signal
  • Inference: we start from pure N(0,I)\mathcal{N}(0, I)N(0,I)—no signal at all

The model was never trained on the exact distribution it encounters at the start of sampling. This mismatch can cause subtle artifacts, especially in color distribution and global structure.

The fix (Lin et al., 2023): enforce αˉT=0\bar\alpha_T = 0αˉT​=0 exactly, so SNR(T)=0\text{SNR}(T) = 0SNR(T)=0. Now the last timestep during training uses pure noise, matching the inference starting point perfectly.

Definition
Zero terminal SNR means enforcing αˉT=0\bar\alpha_T = 0αˉT​=0 so that xTx_TxT​ at the final timestep is pure noise N(0,I)\mathcal{N}(0, I)N(0,I), exactly matching the inference starting distribution. This eliminates the train-test distribution gap.

There's a catch: when αˉT=0\bar\alpha_T = 0αˉT​=0, ε\varepsilonε-prediction becomes undefined at t=Tt = Tt=T because the conversion x0=(xt−1−αˉT⋅ε)/αˉTx_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_T} \cdot \varepsilon) / \sqrt{\bar\alpha_T}x0​=(xt​−1−αˉT​​⋅ε)/αˉT​​ requires dividing by zero. This is one of the key reasons vvv-prediction pairs naturally with zero terminal SNR—the vvv formulation remains well-defined across the entire range.

Continuous-Time Schedules

All our schedules so far use discrete timesteps t∈{0,1,…,T−1}t \in \{0, 1, \ldots, T-1\}t∈{0,1,…,T−1}. But we can also define schedules over continuous time t∈[0,1]t \in [0, 1]t∈[0,1], where αˉ(t)\bar\alpha(t)αˉ(t) is a smooth function:

  • Training: sample t∼U(0,1)t \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 1)t∼U(0,1) uniformly
  • Inference: choose any set of timesteps t1>t2>⋯>tNt_1 > t_2 > \cdots > t_Nt1​>t2​>⋯>tN​ with any NNN

The big advantage: you can use arbitrary step counts at inference without modifying the schedule. Train once, sample with 10 steps or 1000 steps. This is the natural formulation for flow matching (Lecture 13), where the continuous-time perspective simplifies everything.

Log-SNR Linear Schedule

A schedule that's linear in log-SNR space rather than in β\betaβ or αˉ\bar\alphaαˉ space. Since log⁡SNR(t)\log \text{SNR}(t)logSNR(t) decreases linearly, each timestep represents an equal "difficulty" step for the model. This produces a more uniform training signal—no timesteps are much harder or easier than others.
