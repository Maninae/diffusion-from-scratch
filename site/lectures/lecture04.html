U-Net — Encoder, Decoder, Skip Connections

The architecture that learns to predict noise: progressive downsampling, upsampling with skip connections, and why this U-shaped design is uniquely suited to denoising.

U-Net Encoder-Decoder Skip Connections ResBlock
← Reverse Process & Simplified Loss Time Conditioning & Spatial Attention →

A diffusion model needs a neural network that takes a noisy image and outputs a noise prediction of the exact same shape. That rules out standard classifiers, which compress spatial detail away. We'll build the U-Net from scratch: an encoder that captures what's in the image, a decoder that recovers where things are, and skip connections that carry fine detail across the gap.

This Lecture Is For You If
  • You want to understand the neural network that predicts noise
  • You know what convolutions and residual connections are

4.1 The Architecture Problem

In Lecture 3, we derived the training objective for diffusion models: predict the noise $\varepsilon$ that was added to a clean image. The network takes a noisy image of shape $(B, 1, 28, 28)$ and must output a noise prediction of the exact same shape. But what neural network should we use?

A plain CNN could work in theory. Stack some convolutions, maybe some pooling layers, and output a prediction.

But there's a fundamental tension: convolutions with pooling compress spatial information — great for classification ("is this a 7?"), terrible for pixel-level prediction ("what noise was added to this specific pixel?"). Through successive convolutions, fine spatial detail — edges, textures, exact positions — gets progressively destroyed.

We need an architecture that can reason at multiple scales simultaneously: high-level understanding (what digit is this?) and fine spatial detail (where exactly are the edges?). That architecture is the U-Net.

Key Insight
Denoising is fundamentally different from classification. Classification compresses an image into a single label. Denoising must produce a full image — every pixel matters. The architecture must preserve spatial detail while still understanding global structure.

4.2 From Segmentation to Generation

The U-Net was originally designed by Ronneberger et al. (2015) for biomedical image segmentation, labeling every pixel in a microscopy image as "cell" or "background." That task has the same structural requirement as denoising: the output must be the same resolution as the input, and every pixel needs a prediction.

The key insight was simple but powerful: combine high-level semantics from deep layers with fine spatial detail from shallow layers. An encoder compresses the image (capturing what's in it), a decoder expands it back (recovering where things are), and skip connections carry detail directly from encoder to decoder at each resolution level.

What Changed for Diffusion

When Ho et al. (2020) adopted the U-Net for DDPM, they made several modifications:

  • GroupNorm instead of BatchNorm — BatchNorm's statistics depend on batch size, which varies during sampling. GroupNorm computes statistics per-sample, making it stable regardless of batch size.
  • SiLU activation (also called Swish) instead of ReLU — smoother gradients, slightly better performance in practice.
  • Time conditioning — the network needs to know what noise level it's denoising (covered in Lecture 5).
  • Attention layers — self-attention at low resolutions for capturing global structure (also Lecture 5).

The core encoder-decoder-skip architecture, however, is unchanged. Let's trace through each piece.

4.3 The Encoder Path — Progressive Downsampling

The encoder's job is to progressively compress the spatial dimensions while increasing the channel depth. Think of it as building a hierarchy of features: early layers detect simple patterns (edges, textures), deeper layers detect complex structure (digit shapes, global composition).

Structure of Each Encoder Level

Each level in the encoder contains:

  • Two ResBlocks — each applies Conv → GroupNorm → SiLU → Conv with a residual connection. Channels stay constant within a level.
  • Optional attention — at lower resolutions where the token count is manageable.
  • Downsample — a stride-2 convolution that halves spatial dimensions. The channel increase happens at the transition to the next level (the first conv of the next level's ResBlock projects to the new channel count).

Tensor Shapes Through the Encoder

With MNIST's 28×28 grayscale images and a channel progression of $1 \to 64 \to 128 \to 256$, here's exactly what happens to a batch of images:

Input:          (B, 1, 28, 28)    — raw noisy image
Initial conv:   (B, 64, 28, 28)   — project to base channels

Level 1 ResBlocks:  (B, 64, 28, 28)   — two ResBlocks, channels stay at 64
Level 1 Downsample: (B, 64, 14, 14)   — stride-2 conv halves spatial dims

Level 2 ResBlocks:  (B, 128, 14, 14)  — first ResBlock projects 64 → 128
Level 2 Downsample: (B, 128, 7, 7)    — stride-2 conv halves again

Level 3 (Bottleneck): (B, 256, 7, 7)  — first ResBlock projects 128 → 256
                                          no downsample — this is the deepest point

Notice the trade-off: spatial resolution decreases ($28 \to 14 \to 7$) while channel count increases ($64 \to 128 \to 256$). The total information capacity stays roughly constant, but the kind of information changes.

Early layers have high spatial resolution with few channels (where are the edges?). Deep layers have low spatial resolution with many channels (what abstract features are present?).

DownBlock — One Encoder Level
class DownBlock:
    def forward(x, time_emb):
        h = resblock_1(x, time_emb)    # (B, C, H, W) → (B, C, H, W)
        h = resblock_2(h, time_emb)    # same channels
        skip = h                        # save for decoder!
        h = downsample(h)               # (B, C, H, W) → (B, C, H/2, W/2)
        return h, skip

That skip = h line is crucial: we're saving the feature map before downsampling, at full resolution. The decoder will need it.

4.4 The Decoder Path — Upsampling + Skip Concatenation

The decoder mirrors the encoder: it progressively increases spatial resolution while decreasing channels. But unlike a simple mirror, the decoder has a superpower: at each level, it receives the corresponding encoder feature map via a skip connection.

Upsampling Strategy

The decoder uses nearest-neighbor interpolation followed by a convolution rather than transposed convolution (also called "deconvolution"). Why? Transposed convolutions are notorious for producing checkerboard artifacts, grid-like patterns caused by uneven overlap in the transposed operation. Nearest-neighbor upsampling followed by a regular convolution avoids this entirely.

Skip Concatenation — The Channel Math

At each decoder level, here's what happens:

  1. Upsample the decoder features — double the spatial dimensions
  2. Concatenate with the encoder skip connection along the channel dimension
  3. ResBlocks reduce the channel count back to the target

The channel math after concatenation: if the decoder has $C_\text{dec}$ channels and the skip has $C_\text{enc}$ channels, concatenation produces $C_\text{dec} + C_\text{enc}$ channels. The subsequent ResBlock's first convolution reduces this to the target channel count.

Tensor Shapes Through the Decoder

Bottleneck:     (B, 256, 7, 7)

Upsample:       (B, 256, 14, 14)          — nearest-neighbor + conv
+ Skip concat:  (B, 256+128, 14, 14)      = (B, 384, 14, 14)
ResBlocks:      (B, 128, 14, 14)           — convs reduce 384 → 128

Upsample:       (B, 128, 28, 28)
+ Skip concat:  (B, 128+64, 28, 28)       = (B, 192, 28, 28)
ResBlocks:      (B, 64, 28, 28)            — convs reduce 192 → 64

Final conv:     (B, 1, 28, 28)             — same shape as input!

The output has exactly the same shape as the input. It's the predicted noise $\varepsilon_\theta$ — the network's best guess of what noise was added.

UpBlock — One Decoder Level
class UpBlock:
    def forward(x, skip, time_emb):
        x = upsample(x)                # (B, C, H, W) → (B, C, 2H, 2W)
        x = concat(x, skip, dim=1)     # concat along channels
        x = resblock_1(x, time_emb)    # reduce channel count
        x = resblock_2(x, time_emb)
        return x

4.5 Why Skip Connections Are Critical

Skip connections are the feature that makes U-Net a U-Net. Without them, you'd have a vanilla encoder-decoder, and the results would be dramatically worse. Let's understand why.

Without Skip Connections

Without skips, the decoder must reconstruct all spatial detail from the bottleneck alone. With 28×28 inputs and two downsampling stages, that bottleneck is $(B, 256, 7, 7)$, a 7×7 spatial grid. Every fine detail of the original 28×28 image (exact edge positions, subtle textures, thin strokes) must be encoded in those 49 spatial positions. Information gets crushed.

The result: blurry, smooth noise predictions. The network captures the rough structure but loses the fine details that matter for sharp denoising.

With Skip Connections

With skips, fine-grained spatial information flows directly from encoder to decoder at every resolution level. The decoder doesn't need to recover spatial detail from the bottleneck; it receives it on a silver platter via the skip connections. The bottleneck can focus exclusively on high-level reasoning (what objects are present, what the global structure looks like), while the skips carry the low-level detail (exact edge positions, textures).

Definition
Skip connection. A direct pathway that copies feature maps from an encoder level to the corresponding decoder level, bypassing the bottleneck. In DDPM's U-Net, skips use concatenation (stacking channels) rather than addition, giving the network maximum flexibility in how it combines the two information streams.

Concatenation vs. Addition

There are two ways to combine the skip with the decoder features:

  • Concatenation (DDPM) — stack along the channel dimension, then let convolutions learn how to merge. More expressive: the network has separate access to both representations.
  • Addition (some architectures) — element-wise sum. Simpler and uses fewer parameters, but forces the two representations into the same space.

The Gradient Highway

Skip connections have a second benefit beyond spatial detail: they provide a shortcut for gradient flow during training. This is the same principle behind ResNets: gradients can flow directly from decoder loss back to early encoder layers without passing through the bottleneck. Training is faster and more stable.

Note
Memory cost. Skip connections require storing all encoder feature maps during the forward pass. For a large model with high-resolution inputs, these stored feature maps are often the dominant memory cost during training, more than the model parameters themselves.

4.6 The Middle Block (Bottleneck)

Between the encoder and decoder sits the middle block, operating at the lowest spatial resolution. With 28×28 inputs, that's 7×7. Its structure is:

ResBlock → Self-Attention → ResBlock

This is where global reasoning happens. At 7×7 resolution, the self-attention layer processes only $49$ tokens (spatial positions). The attention matrix is $49 \times 49 = 2{,}401$ entries, trivially small. But the receptive field is global: every spatial position can attend to every other, capturing long-range dependencies that convolutions miss.

You can think of the middle block as the "brain" of the U-Net, the place where the network considers the image as a whole before the decoder begins reconstructing spatial detail.

4.7 Assembling the Full U-Net

Now let's put all the pieces together. The full forward pass proceeds in five stages:

  1. Initial convolution: project from image channels to base channels — $(B, 1, 28, 28) \to (B, 64, 28, 28)$
  2. Encoder: $N$ DownBlocks, each producing a skip connection and halving spatial dims
  3. Middle block: ResBlock → Attention → ResBlock at the lowest resolution
  4. Decoder: $N$ UpBlocks, each consuming a skip connection (in LIFO order) and doubling spatial dims
  5. Final layer: GroupNorm → SiLU → Conv back to image channels — $(B, 64, 28, 28) \to (B, 1, 28, 28)$
Key Insight
The skip connections are consumed in LIFO order (last in, first out). The deepest encoder level's skip connects to the first decoder level, because the decoder mirrors the encoder; it starts at the lowest resolution and works upward.
UNet — Complete Forward Pass
class UNet:
    def forward(x, t):
        # Time embedding (detailed in Lecture 5)
        t_emb = time_mlp(sinusoidal_embedding(t))

        # Initial projection
        h = init_conv(x)                    # (B, 1, 28, 28) → (B, 64, 28, 28)

        # Encoder — save skip connections
        skips = []
        for down_block in encoder:
            h, skip = down_block(h, t_emb)
            skips.append(skip)

        # Middle block
        h = mid_block(h, t_emb)             # (B, 256, 7, 7) → (B, 256, 7, 7)

        # Decoder — use skips in reverse order
        for up_block in decoder:
            skip = skips.pop()               # LIFO!
            h = up_block(h, skip, t_emb)

        # Output projection
        return final_conv(h)                # (B, 64, 28, 28) → (B, 1, 28, 28)

The output has the same shape as the input. It represents the predicted noise $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ — the model's estimate of what Gaussian noise was added to the clean image to produce $x_t$ at timestep $t$.

4.8 The Network Blocks: ResBlock and Alternatives

The ResBlock is the fundamental building block. The U-Net is essentially built by stacking these. Let's look at what happens inside one:

ResBlock forward pass:
  h = GroupNorm(x)                    # normalize
  h = SiLU(h)                         # activate
  h = Conv2d(h)                       # first convolution
  h = h + time_projection             # inject time info (Lecture 5)
  h = GroupNorm(h)                    # normalize again
  h = SiLU(h)                         # activate
  h = Dropout(h)                      # regularize
  h = Conv2d(h)                       # second convolution
  return h + shortcut(x)              # residual connection

The residual connection (h + shortcut(x)) is critical. If the input and output have the same number of channels, the shortcut is just the identity. If channels change (e.g., at level transitions), a 1×1 convolution projects the input to match.

The Math

In compact notation:

$$\text{ResBlock}(x) = \text{Conv}\big(\text{SiLU}(\text{GN}(\text{Conv}(\text{SiLU}(\text{GN}(x))) + t_\text{proj}))\big) + \text{shortcut}(x)$$

where GN is GroupNorm and $t_\text{proj}$ is the projected time embedding broadcast over spatial dimensions.

This two-conv ResBlock is the simplest member of a family of residual block designs. DDPM uses it because the U-Net is modest-sized and simplicity wins. But as architectures scale up or target constrained hardware, the block design becomes a critical lever for trading off compute, memory, and expressiveness. The three main alternatives are worth knowing.

Bottleneck Blocks

The original ResNet paper introduced two block designs. ResNet-18 and ResNet-34 use two 3×3 convolutions, just like our U-Net. ResNet-50 and deeper variants use a bottleneck pattern instead: 1×1 conv (compress channels) → 3×3 conv (spatial processing) → 1×1 conv (expand channels back).

The motivation is computational. A 3×3 conv with 256 input and 256 output channels requires $256 \times 256 \times 9 = 589{,}824$ multiply-adds per spatial position. By compressing to 64 channels first, the expensive 3×3 conv operates on only 64 channels, cutting the cost by roughly 4× while preserving the same input/output dimensionality. This is what enabled ResNet to scale to 152 layers without the compute budget exploding.

DDPM's U-Net doesn't use bottleneck blocks. At 28×28 resolution with 64–256 channels, the two-conv block is already cheap enough, and the bottleneck's channel compression would reduce the block's representational capacity for marginal compute savings.

Inverted Bottleneck Blocks

The bottleneck block compresses, processes, then expands. MobileNetV2 flipped this order: expand channels with a 1×1 conv, apply a depthwise 3×3 conv (one filter per channel, no cross-channel mixing), then compress back with a 1×1 conv. This is the inverted bottleneck.

The design puts most of the computation into the depthwise conv, which is far cheaper than a standard conv because it processes each channel independently. The 1×1 convs handle cross-channel mixing at minimal cost. The result is comparable accuracy to standard blocks at a fraction of the FLOPs, which is why this pattern dominates efficient architectures like EfficientNet and ConvNeXt.

Pointwise and Depthwise Convolutions

Two building blocks appear repeatedly in the patterns above. A pointwise convolution (1×1 conv) mixes information across channels at each spatial position without looking at neighboring pixels. It's a per-pixel linear transformation, cheap and useful for changing channel counts or combining features.

A depthwise convolution applies a separate spatial filter to each channel independently. It captures local spatial patterns (edges, textures) without any cross-channel interaction. Together, a depthwise conv followed by a pointwise conv forms a depthwise separable convolution, which factorizes a standard convolution into two cheaper operations. For a $k \times k$ conv with $C$ input and $C$ output channels, the separable version reduces multiply-adds by roughly a factor of $C / k^2$.

For our MNIST U-Net, none of this matters. The model is small, trains in minutes, and standard 3×3 convolutions are the right choice. These alternatives become important when you scale to high-resolution generation (256×256 and beyond), where the channel counts reach 512–1024 and every redundant multiply-add adds up. If you later encounter architectures like Efficient U-Net or ConvNeXt-based diffusion models, you'll recognize these blocks as the building blocks they're assembled from.

4.9 Key Operations — Summary

Here are the four core operations that define the U-Net's data flow:

Downsampling

Stride-2 convolution:

$$(B, C, H, W) \xrightarrow{\text{Conv2d}(s=2)} (B, C_\text{out}, H/2, W/2)$$

Upsampling

Nearest-neighbor interpolation followed by convolution:

$$(B, C, H, W) \xrightarrow{\text{interpolate}} (B, C, 2H, 2W) \xrightarrow{\text{Conv2d}} (B, C_\text{out}, 2H, 2W)$$

Skip Concatenation

Channel-wise concatenation of decoder and encoder features:

$$(B, C_\text{dec}, H, W) \;\|\; (B, C_\text{enc}, H, W) \;\longrightarrow\; (B, C_\text{dec} + C_\text{enc}, H, W)$$

Residual Connection

$$\text{output} = f(x) + \text{shortcut}(x)$$

where $\text{shortcut}(x) = x$ if channels match, or $\text{shortcut}(x) = \text{Conv}_{1 \times 1}(x)$ if they don't.

4.11 Looking Ahead

We now have the architectural skeleton of our denoising network: the U-shaped encoder-decoder with skip connections that can take a noisy image and predict the noise. But there's a critical piece missing: the network has no idea what noise level it's working with.

Denoising at $t = 50$ (slight haze over a clean image) is a fundamentally different task from denoising at $t = 950$ (almost pure static). In Lecture 5, we'll add time conditioning via sinusoidal embeddings and spatial self-attention for capturing long-range dependencies. These two additions will complete the architecture, making it ready for training.
