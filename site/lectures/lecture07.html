Sampling — DDPM, DDIM & Beyond

Starting from pure noise and iteratively denoising to generate images: the stochastic DDPM reverse process, deterministic DDIM, and why fewer steps can work just as well.

Training taught the model to predict noise. Now we run the process in reverse to generate images from scratch. We'll implement DDPM's full 1000-step stochastic sampler, then see how DDIM reformulates the same model as a deterministic ODE, cutting the step count dramatically while unlocking smooth interpolation and the probability flow perspective that connects diffusion to continuous dynamics.

DDPM Sampling — Algorithm 2

Training taught the model to predict noise at any timestep. Sampling reverses the process: start from pure Gaussian noise $\mathbf{x}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ and iteratively denoise, one step at a time, until we arrive at a clean image $\mathbf{x}_0$.

Algorithm 2 — DDPM Sampling (Ho et al., 2020)
  1. $\mathbf{x}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ — start from pure noise
  2. for $t = T, T-1, \ldots, 1$ do
  3. $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ if $t > 1$, else $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$
  4. $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta = \text{model}(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$ — predict noise
  5. $\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(\mathbf{x}_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta\right)$ — compute mean
  6. $\mathbf{x}_{t-1} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta + \sigma_t \mathbf{z}$ — sample
  7. end for
  8. return $\mathbf{x}_0$

Step by Step

At each timestep, the model sees a noisy image $\mathbf{x}_t$ and outputs its best guess of what noise was added: $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$. We then use this prediction to compute the posterior mean — the expected value of $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ given $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the predicted noise:

$$\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(\mathbf{x}_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\right)$$

This formula comes directly from Lecture 3 (Section 3.5). The mean tells us: "given the current noisy image and our noise prediction, here's the most likely slightly-less-noisy image." Then we add a controlled amount of fresh noise $\sigma_t \mathbf{z}$ and repeat.

Two Variance Choices

The standard deviation $\sigma_t$ controls how much fresh noise we inject at each step. DDPM offers two options:

  • $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$ — the DDPM default. Simple, works well.
  • $\sigma_t^2 = \tilde{\beta}_t = \frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \cdot \beta_t$ — the true posterior variance. Often produces slightly better samples.

Both are valid. The difference is subtle for MNIST; it matters more for higher-resolution images.

Pseudocode

def ddpm_sample(model, shape, schedule, T=1000): x = randn(shape).to(device) # x_T ~ N(0, I) for t in reversed(range(1, T+1)): noise_pred = model(x, t) # predict noise # Compute posterior mean coeff1 = 1 / sqrt(schedule.alphas[t]) coeff2 = schedule.betas[t] / sqrt(1 - schedule.alphas_cumprod[t]) mean = coeff1 * (x - coeff2 * noise_pred) # Add noise (except at final step) if t > 1: noise = randn_like(x) sigma = sqrt(schedule.betas[t]) x = mean + sigma * noise else: x = mean return x # x_0 prediction
Example
Concrete numbers for MNIST. With $T = 1000$ and a cosine schedule, the model runs 1,000 sequential forward passes through the U-Net. On an M1 MacBook (MPS), this takes about 15–30 seconds per batch of 16 images. On a GPU, it's 2–5 seconds. This is why accelerated sampling (Section 7.4) matters.

Why We Add Noise During Sampling

This is the most counterintuitive part of diffusion sampling. We're trying to remove noise, so why do we add more noise at each step?

Stochastic Sampling as Error Correction

The noise injection serves as a form of error correction. The model's noise prediction at each step isn't perfect; it's an approximation.

Without the injected noise, these small errors compound across 1,000 steps, gradually steering the sample away from the data distribution. The fresh noise at each step gives the model a chance to "re-randomize" and correct its trajectory.

Think of it like this: if you're navigating through fog with a slightly miscalibrated compass, occasionally re-randomizing your direction (within a shrinking radius) keeps you closer to the target than blindly following the compass for 1,000 steps.

Diversity

The noise also produces sample diversity. Starting from the same $\mathbf{x}_T$, different noise injections at each step lead to different final images. Run DDPM twice from the same starting noise and you'll get two different digits. This stochasticity is what makes the model a proper generative model; it doesn't memorize a fixed mapping.

The Deterministic Alternative

What happens if we set $\sigma_t = 0$ — no noise at all? We get a deterministic mapping from $\mathbf{x}_T$ to $\mathbf{x}_0$. The same starting noise always produces the same image. This loses diversity but gains something valuable: reproducibility and interpolation. And as we'll see next, it also enables step-skipping.

DDIM — Deterministic Sampling

DDPM sampling requires all $T = 1000$ steps because the noise injection at each step is calibrated for that specific schedule. You can't just skip steps; the noise magnitudes would be wrong, and sample quality collapses.

DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models, Song, Meng & Ermon, 2020) solves this by reformulating the reverse process with a tunable noise parameter $\eta$:

  • $\eta = 1$: equivalent to DDPM (full stochastic sampling)
  • $\eta = 0$: fully deterministic — no noise injection at all
  • $0 < \eta < 1$: interpolation between the two

The DDIM Update Rule

Instead of computing a posterior mean and adding noise, DDIM takes a different approach. At each step:

  1. Predict $\mathbf{x}_0$ directly from the current $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the noise prediction: $$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 = \frac{\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \cdot \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}}$$
  2. Compute the "direction pointing to $\mathbf{x}_t$": $$\text{dir} = \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1} - \sigma_t^2} \cdot \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta$$
  3. Compute the noise magnitude: $$\sigma_t = \eta \cdot \sqrt{\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}} \cdot \sqrt{1-\frac{\bar{\alpha}_t}{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}}$$
  4. Take the DDIM step: $$\mathbf{x}_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + \text{dir} + \sigma_t \cdot \mathbf{z}$$
Definition
DDIM (Song, Meng & Ermon, 2020). A non-Markovian sampling process that shares the same marginal distributions $q(\mathbf{x}_t | \mathbf{x}_0)$ as DDPM. Because the forward marginals are identical, a model trained with DDPM can be used directly for DDIM sampling with no retraining required. DDIM is a drop-in replacement for the DDPM sampler.

Pseudocode

def ddim_sample(model, shape, schedule, num_steps=50, eta=0.0): # Select timestep subsequence timesteps = linspace(0, T-1, num_steps, dtype=int) x = randn(shape).to(device) # x_T ~ N(0, I) for i in reversed(range(len(timesteps))): t = timesteps[i] t_prev = timesteps[i-1] if i > 0 else 0 noise_pred = model(x, t) # Predict x_0 alpha_bar_t = schedule.alphas_cumprod[t] alpha_bar_prev = schedule.alphas_cumprod[t_prev] x0_pred = (x - sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t) * noise_pred) / sqrt(alpha_bar_t) # Compute sigma sigma = eta * sqrt((1 - alpha_bar_prev) / (1 - alpha_bar_t)) * \ sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t / alpha_bar_prev) # Direction pointing to x_t direction = sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_prev - sigma**2) * noise_pred # DDIM step noise = randn_like(x) if t > 0 else 0 x = sqrt(alpha_bar_prev) * x0_pred + direction + sigma * noise return x

Fewer Steps — Accelerated Sampling

This is the real payoff of DDIM. When $\eta = 0$, the sampling trajectory is a smooth, deterministic curve from $\mathbf{x}_T$ to $\mathbf{x}_0$. Smooth curves can be traversed at any resolution; you don't need to visit every one of 1,000 points along the way.

Timestep Sub-Selection

Instead of stepping through every timestep $[999, 998, 997, \ldots, 1, 0]$, we select a subset: $[999, 979, 959, \ldots, 19, 0]$. The model evaluates at each selected timestep and skips the ones in between. Two common strategies:

  • Uniform spacing: timesteps = linspace(0, T-1, num_steps) — evenly distributed across the schedule
  • Quadratic spacing: more steps concentrated near $t = 0$, where fine details emerge and accuracy matters most

Quality vs. Speed

Here's the tradeoff in practice:

  • 1000 steps — best quality (the DDPM baseline)
  • 200 steps — nearly identical quality, hard to tell the difference
  • 50 steps — minimal quality loss. This is the sweet spot.
  • 20 steps — visible degradation but still recognizable digits
  • 10 steps — significant quality loss, blurry outputs

Going from 1,000 to 50 steps is a 20× speedup with nearly the same output quality.

This is why DDIM was a breakthrough. It made diffusion models practical for interactive use.

Key Insight
DDIM doesn't require a different model or any retraining. It's purely a change to the sampling procedure. You can train once with DDPM's Algorithm 1 and sample with either DDPM (1000 steps, stochastic, diverse) or DDIM (50 steps, deterministic, fast) depending on your needs.

Dynamic Thresholding

At each DDIM step, we compute a prediction of $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$. Ideally this prediction stays in the $[-1, 1]$ range (since our training data was normalized there), but in practice it can overshoot, especially at high guidance scales (which we'll encounter in Lecture 9).

Static Clipping

The simplest fix: x0_pred.clamp(-1, 1). This works but can create color banding artifacts when predictions significantly exceed the range.

Dynamic Thresholding (Imagen)

Dynamic thresholding (Saharia et al., 2022) is smarter. At each step, compute the $p$-th percentile of $|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0|$ (typically $p = 99.5$). If that percentile exceeds 1, clip to that value and rescale to $[-1, 1]$. This allows higher guidance scales without saturation artifacts.

For MNIST with standard DDPM/DDIM (no guidance), static clipping is perfectly fine. Dynamic thresholding becomes important in Lecture 9 when we add classifier-free guidance.

The Full Sampling Pipeline

Let's put the complete end-to-end pipeline together, from random noise to displayable images:

  1. Generate starting noise: $\mathbf{x}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ with shape $(B, C, H, W)$
  2. Iterative denoising: Run DDPM (1000 steps) or DDIM (50 steps)
  3. Clamp: $\mathbf{x}_0 = \text{clamp}(\mathbf{x}_0, -1, 1)$ — ensure valid range
  4. Denormalize: $\mathbf{x}_{\text{display}} = (\mathbf{x}_0 + 1) / 2$ — map from $[-1, 1]$ to $[0, 1]$
  5. Display: Show as an image grid using show_images()
# Generate 16 MNIST digits with DDIM (50 steps) with torch.no_grad(): samples = ddim_sample(model, shape=(16, 1, 28, 28), schedule, num_steps=50) samples = samples.clamp(-1, 1) samples = (samples + 1) / 2 # denormalize to [0, 1] show_images(samples, nrow=4)

Stochastic vs. Deterministic: A Side-by-Side

A revealing experiment: take the same starting noise $\mathbf{x}_T$ and generate images with both methods.

  • DDPM (stochastic): Run it three times — you get three different digits each time. The noise injected at each step makes each run unique.
  • DDIM (deterministic, $\eta = 0$): Run it three times — you get the exact same digit every time. The mapping from $\mathbf{x}_T$ to $\mathbf{x}_0$ is fixed.

This determinism is what makes DDIM useful beyond just speed. It enables latent space interpolation: smoothly blend between two starting noises $\mathbf{x}_T^{(1)}$ and $\mathbf{x}_T^{(2)}$ and the generated images smoothly morph between the two outputs.

The Probability Flow ODE Optional / Advanced

If you want to understand why DDIM works — and where the field is heading — this section provides the theoretical perspective. Feel free to skip it on a first reading.

Two Views of the Same Process

There are two equivalent mathematical descriptions of diffusion sampling:

  1. SDE (Stochastic Differential Equation): DDPM sampling. The reverse process includes a random noise term — it's a stochastic trajectory from $\mathbf{x}_T$ to $\mathbf{x}_0$.
  2. ODE (Ordinary Differential Equation): A deterministic trajectory. No randomness — the path from noise to data is a smooth curve defined by the model's predictions.

The ODE form is:

$$d\mathbf{x} = \left[f(\mathbf{x}, t) - \frac{1}{2}g(t)^2 \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p_t(\mathbf{x})\right] dt$$

The key term is the score function $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p_t(\mathbf{x})$ — the gradient of the log-probability of the data at noise level $t$. Our trained model gives us this for free:

$$\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p_t(\mathbf{x}) \approx -\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}$$

In other words, predicting noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ is equivalent to estimating the score function. This connection is profound: it unifies diffusion models with score-based generative models (Song & Ermon, 2019) and explains why DDIM works.

DDIM as Euler Discretization

DDIM with $\eta = 0$ is exactly the Euler method applied to this ODE. And just like in numerical methods, we can use better ODE solvers to get the same quality in fewer steps:

  • Heun's method (second-order): roughly halves the required steps vs. Euler
  • DPM-Solver (Lu et al., 2022): purpose-built for diffusion ODEs, achieves good quality in 10–20 steps
  • DPM-Solver++: further improvements, used in Stable Diffusion

This ODE perspective also connects to flow matching (Lecture 10), which directly learns the velocity field of the ODE rather than deriving it from a noise prediction model.

Key Insight
The progression from DDPM → DDIM → DPM-Solver → flow matching is really a story about better ODE solvers. Each step realizes that the "reverse diffusion" process is an ODE, and applies increasingly sophisticated numerical methods to traverse it efficiently.

Looking Ahead

We can now both train and sample from a diffusion model. But our model generates random digits, and we have no control over what it produces. You might get a 7, a 3, or a 0. There's no way to say "generate a 5."

The next lecture introduces conditioning: feeding class labels, text, or other signals into the model so it generates specific outputs on demand. This is the leap from an interesting mathematical framework to a practical tool: text-to-image generation, class-conditional synthesis, and everything that makes diffusion models useful in the real world.
