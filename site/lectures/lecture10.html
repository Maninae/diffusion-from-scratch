Latent Diffusion & Stable Diffusion

Why pixel-space diffusion breaks at high resolution, how VAEs compress images into a small latent space, and the architecture behind Stable Diffusion.

VAE Latent Space Stable Diffusion Scaling
← Classifier-Free Guidance Prediction Targets & Advanced Schedules →

The diffusion models we've discussed so far work on smaller images, but real-world generation happens at high resolutions such as 512×512 and beyond. Pixel-space diffusion has difficulty scaling to this size, because attention costs explode quadratically with resolution. The solution: compress images into a small latent space with a VAE, then run diffusion in that latent space. This is the architecture behind Stable Diffusion, and it's what made high-resolution generation practical.

This Lecture Is For You If
  • You want to understand how diffusion scales to high-resolution images
  • You know what autoencoders are, or are ready to learn

10.1 The Resolution Bottleneck

Everything we've built so far works well on small images like 28×28 for MNIST or 32x32 for CIFAR-10. But the pixel-space diffusion paradigm hits challenges at higher resolutions. Why is that?

The pixel count at different resolutions tells the story:

  • 28×28×1 (grayscale): 784 values
  • 32×32×3 (CIFAR-10): 3,072 values
  • 256×256×3: 196,608 values
  • 512×512×3: 786,432 values

A U-Net handling 784 values (28×28) runs comfortably. But at 512×512, it's processing 786K pixel values per denoising step, and we may need hundreds of steps. The model size isn't the bottleneck; it's the feature map sizes flowing through the network.

The Attention Problem

Recall from Lecture 5 that attention is O((H×W)2)O((H \times W)^2)O((H×W)2) in spatial dimension. Let's run the numbers:

  • At 32×32: attention operates on 1,024 tokens → ~1M operations per layer. Totally fine.
  • At 256×256: attention operates on 65,536 tokens → ~4 billion operations per layer. Massively greater.

You could avoid attention at high resolutions and rely only on convolutions, but convolutions are local operators which lose the global context from attention blocks that makes our generated images coherent. A face might have perfectly rendered eyes but mismatched ears, because the receptive field of convolutions isn't large enough to coordinate distant features.

You could also try progressive generation: train a low-resolution model, then train a separate super-resolution model. But this requires multiple models, multiple training runs, and information loss at each stage.

Key Insight
The fundamental bottleneck is that pixel space is too large for the operations we need. Rather than making the operations cheaper, we compress the space itself.

10.2 Review: Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)

Since compressing the space is the solution, we need a tool for the job: the variational autoencoder. We present a compact treatment here to understand what it does and why it works.

Encoder and Decoder

A VAE has two halves:

The encoder EEE maps an image xxx to a lower-dimensional latent representation zzz. Commonly, it's a series of convolutional layers with downsampling (stride-2 convolutions or pooling); more modern architectures use ViT- or attention-based blocks as well.

Crucially, the encoder doesn't output a single point in latent space; it outputs two things: a mean μ\muμ and a log-variance log⁡σ2\log \sigma^2logσ2 for a Gaussian distribution over latent codes.

The decoder DDD maps a latent code zzz back to pixel space, producing a reconstructed image x^\hat{x}x^. It's the mirror of the encoder: convolutional layers with upsampling (transposed convolutions or interpolation followed by convolution).

A typical compression ratio: 256×256×3→32×32×4256 \times 256 \times 3 \to 32 \times 32 \times 4256×256×3→32×32×4, which is a 48× compression in the number of values (196,608 down to 4,096).

The "variational" in the name comes from training: the VAE loss combines a reconstruction term (how well the decoder recovers the original image) with a KL regularization term that pushes the encoder's output distribution toward a standard Gaussian. This regularization is what gives the latent space its useful properties.

The VAE compresses high-dimensional input x into a compact latent code z, then reconstructs x̂ ≈ x. The bottleneck forces the network to learn efficient representations.

The Latent Space

What does the latent space actually look like? Because of that KL regularization pushing the distribution toward a standard Gaussian, the latent space has useful structure:

  • Nearby points in latent space map to similar images after decoding
  • Interpolation between two latent codes produces a smooth visual transition, not a jarring pixel-space average
  • The space is dense: most random samples from N(0,I)\mathcal{N}(0, I)N(0,I) decode to plausible-looking images (not noise or artifacts)

This last property is exactly why the VAE latent space is a good place to run diffusion. The diffusion model's job at the end of sampling is to produce a clean latent code z0z_0z0​, and the VAE decoder can turn any reasonable latent code into a good image.

A simplified 2D cross-section of latent space. Real latent spaces are much higher-dimensional (typically 4×64×64 for Stable Diffusion), but the principle holds: VAE training creates smooth, overlapping regions where nearby points decode to semantically similar images.

10.3 Latent Diffusion — The Key Insight

The key idea (Rombach et al., 2022): instead of running diffusion in pixel space, run it in the VAE's latent space. The recipe has three steps:

The Latent Diffusion Recipe
  1. Train a VAE on images—learn encoder EEE and decoder DDD. Then freeze it completely.
  2. Encode all training images: z0=E(x)z_0 = E(x)z0​=E(x)—you now have a dataset of latent codes instead of pixel images.
  3. Train a diffusion model on z0z_0z0​ instead of xxx. The U-Net is smaller because it operates on 64×64×464 \times 64 \times 464×64×4 latents instead of 512×512×3512 \times 512 \times 3512×512×3 pixels.

At inference, the process runs in reverse:

  1. Sample zT∼N(0,I)z_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)zT​∼N(0,I) in latent space—shape (1,4,64,64)(1, 4, 64, 64)(1,4,64,64)
  2. Denoise: zT→zT−1→⋯→z0z_T \to z_{T-1} \to \cdots \to z_0zT​→zT−1​→⋯→z0​ using the diffusion model (same DDPM/DDIM sampling we learned in Lectures 3 and 7)
  3. Decode: x=D(z0)x = D(z_0)x=D(z0​)—back to pixel space, shape (1,3,512,512)(1, 3, 512, 512)(1,3,512,512)

Why This Works So Well

The two stages specialize in different things:

  • The VAE handles perceptual compression: it strips away imperceptible high-frequency detail that humans don't notice. The pixel-level reconstruction doesn't need to be perfect; it just needs to look right.
  • The diffusion model handles semantic generation: it only needs to model the meaningful structure of images (shapes, textures, compositions), not every individual pixel.

The compression from 512×512×3512 \times 512 \times 3512×512×3 to 64×64×464 \times 64 \times 464×64×4 is a 48× reduction in the number of values. The U-Net is proportionally smaller, attention is affordable (operating on 64×64=4,09664 \times 64 = 4{,}09664×64=4,096 tokens instead of 512×512=262,144512 \times 512 = 262{,}144512×512=262,144), and training is 10–100× cheaper than pixel-space diffusion at the same resolution.

Pseudocode

# Stage 1: Train VAE (done once, then frozen) vae = train_vae(image_dataset) # Stage 2: Encode all training images into latent space latent_dataset = [vae.encode(x) for x in image_dataset] # Stage 3: Train diffusion model on latents # Exactly the same training loop as Lecture 6, but on z_0 instead of x diffusion_model = train_diffusion(latent_dataset) # Inference: generate new images z_T = torch.randn(batch_size, 4, 64, 64) # start from noise in latent space z_0 = ddim_sample(diffusion_model, z_T, schedule, num_steps=50) images = vae.decode(z_0) # decode back to pixel space: (B, 3, 512, 512)

Notice how little changes from the pixel-space pipeline in Lectures 3–7. The forward process, training loop, and sampling algorithm are identical; we've just swapped the data from pixels to latents.

10.4 The Stable Diffusion Architecture

Stable Diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022) is latent diffusion plus text conditioning. The architecture has three main components, each trained separately:

Component 1: The VAE

A KL-regularized autoencoder that handles the pixel↔latent conversion:

  • Encoder: 512×512×3→64×64×4512 \times 512 \times 3 \to 64 \times 64 \times 4512×512×3→64×64×4 (8× spatial compression, ~48× total)
  • Decoder: 64×64×4→512×512×364 \times 64 \times 4 \to 512 \times 512 \times 364×64×4→512×512×3
  • Trained separately on large image datasets, then frozen

Component 2: The U-Net

The diffusion model operates entirely in the 64×6464 \times 6464×64 latent space. It's the same U-Net architecture we built in Lecture 4, with one addition: cross-attention layers that let the model attend to text embeddings. At each attention block, the image features are queries, and the text embeddings are keys and values. This is how the model "listens" to the text prompt.

The U-Net is time-conditioned (sinusoidal embeddings, just like Lectures 4–5) and much smaller than a pixel-space model at the same output resolution would be.

Component 3: The Text Encoder

Converts a text prompt into a sequence of embeddings that the U-Net can attend to:

  • SD 1.5: CLIP ViT-L/14—77 tokens, 768-dimensional embeddings
  • SDXL: dual text encoders (CLIP + OpenCLIP) for richer representations
  • SD 3: T5-XXL for more powerful text understanding

The text encoder is pretrained (on image-text pairs for CLIP, on text for T5) and frozen during diffusion training. Only the U-Net's weights are trained.

The Full Inference Pipeline

Stable Diffusion Inference
  1. Text encoding: prompt → text encoder → text embeddings with shape (seq_len, d_text)
  2. Sample noise: zT∼N(0,I)z_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)zT​∼N(0,I) with shape (1, 4, 64, 64)
  3. Iterative denoising (e.g., 50 DDIM steps):
    • U-Net predicts noise, conditioned on timestep ttt and text embeddings (via cross-attention)
    • Classifier-free guidance (Lecture 9) combines conditional and unconditional predictions
  4. Decode: x=D(z0)x = D(z_0)x=D(z0​) → shape (1, 3, 512, 512)
  5. Denormalize from [−1,1][-1, 1][−1,1] to [0,255][0, 255][0,255] and display

Every component we've studied in this course shows up: the U-Net (Lecture 4), noise schedules (Lecture 5), the training loop (Lecture 6), DDIM sampling (Lecture 7), and classifier-free guidance (Lecture 9). Latent diffusion ties them all together into a system that actually works at scale.

10.5 The Evolution of Stable Diffusion

Stable Diffusion has evolved rapidly since its release, with each version improving text encoders, model capacity, and the underlying framework:

  • SD 1.5 — the original release. CLIP text encoder, 64×6464 \times 6464×64 latent, ~860M U-Net parameters. ε\varepsilonε-prediction with a linear noise schedule. The first widely adopted open-source image generation model.
  • SD 2.x — switched to OpenCLIP text encoder, adopted v-prediction (we'll cover this in Lecture 11), larger model. Better image quality, especially at higher resolutions.
  • SDXL — dual text encoders for richer text understanding, 128×128128 \times 128128×128 base latent for higher fidelity, plus a separate refiner model. Significantly better prompt adherence.
  • SD 3 — a major architecture shift: replaces the U-Net with a Diffusion Transformer (Lecture 12), uses T5-XXL text encoder, and adopts flow matching (Lecture 13) instead of DDPM. The state of the art.
Key Insight
The trend across SD versions is clear: better text encoders, larger models, and more advanced training frameworks. But the core latent diffusion insight—compress with a VAE, diffuse in latent space—remains unchanged.
