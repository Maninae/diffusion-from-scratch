Latent Diffusion & Stable Diffusion

Why pixel-space diffusion breaks at high resolution, how VAEs compress images into a small latent space, and the architecture behind Stable Diffusion.

VAE Latent Space Stable Diffusion Scaling
← Classifier-Free Guidance Prediction Targets & Advanced Schedules →

The diffusion model we built works on 28×28 images, but real-world generation happens at 512×512 and beyond. Pixel-space diffusion can't scale there because attention costs explode quadratically with resolution. The solution: compress images into a small latent space with a VAE, then run diffusion in that latent space. This is the architecture behind Stable Diffusion, and it's what made high-resolution generation practical.

This Lecture Is For You If
  • You want to understand how diffusion scales to high-resolution images
  • You know what autoencoders are, or are ready to learn

10.1 The Resolution Bottleneck

Everything we've built so far works well on small images like 28×28 MNIST. But real-world image generation happens at 512×512 or higher. Let's see why that's a problem.

The pixel count at different resolutions tells the story:

  • 28×28×1 (MNIST): 784 values
  • 32×32×3 (CIFAR-10): 3,072 values
  • 256×256×3: 196,608 values
  • 512×512×3: 786,432 values

A U-Net handling 784 values (28×28 MNIST) runs comfortably. But at 512×512, it's processing 786K pixel values per denoising step, and we need hundreds of steps. The model size isn't the bottleneck; it's the feature map sizes flowing through the network.

The Attention Problem

Recall from Lecture 5 that attention is $O((H \times W)^2)$ in spatial dimension. Let's run the numbers:

  • At 32×32: attention operates on 1,024 tokens → ~1M operations per layer. Totally fine.
  • At 256×256: attention operates on 65,536 tokens → ~4 billion operations per layer. Impractical.

You could avoid attention at high resolutions and rely only on convolutions, but convolutions are local operators. Without attention, the model loses the global context that makes generated images coherent.

A face might have perfectly rendered eyes but mismatched ears, because the receptive field of convolutions isn't large enough to coordinate distant features.

You could also try progressive generation: train a low-resolution model, then train a separate super-resolution model. But this requires multiple models, multiple training runs, and information loss at each stage.

Key Insight
The fundamental bottleneck is that pixel space is too large for the operations we need. Rather than making the operations cheaper, we compress the space itself.

10.2 VAE Primer

If you took CS231N, you've seen variational autoencoders before. We need a compact treatment here because the VAE is a critical component of latent diffusion, not the main event, but you need to understand what it does and why it works.

Let's go deeper than a refresher but keep the focus on what matters for diffusion.

Encoder and Decoder

A VAE has two halves:

The encoder $E$ maps an image $x$ to a lower-dimensional latent representation $z$. Architecturally, it's a series of convolutional layers with downsampling (stride-2 convolutions or pooling).

Crucially, the encoder doesn't output a single point in latent space; it outputs two things: a mean $\mu$ and a log-variance $\log \sigma^2$ for a Gaussian distribution over latent codes.

The decoder $D$ maps a latent code $z$ back to pixel space, producing a reconstructed image $\hat{x}$. It's the mirror of the encoder: convolutional layers with upsampling (transposed convolutions or interpolation followed by convolution).

A typical compression ratio: $256 \times 256 \times 3 \to 32 \times 32 \times 4$, which is a 48× compression in the number of values (196,608 down to 4,096).

The ELBO Loss

The VAE is trained with two competing objectives, balanced into a single loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{VAE}} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[\|x - D(z)\|^2\big]}_{\text{reconstruction loss}} + \beta \cdot \underbrace{D_{\text{KL}}\big(q(z|x) \;\|\; \mathcal{N}(0, I)\big)}_{\text{KL regularization}}$$
  • Reconstruction loss — how well the decoder reconstructs the original image from the latent code. Lower is better: the encoder should preserve enough information for faithful reconstruction.
  • KL regularization — keeps the learned latent distribution close to a standard Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$. This prevents the encoder from "cheating" by mapping each image to a unique, isolated point. Instead, it forces the latent space to be smooth and continuous.
  • $\beta$ controls the trade-off: higher $\beta$ produces a smoother latent space but lower reconstruction quality. Lower $\beta$ gives sharper reconstructions but a less regular latent space.

The Reparameterization Trick

Sampling $z$ from the distribution $q(z|x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ isn't differentiable; you can't backpropagate through a random sampling operation. The reparameterization trick sidesteps this:

$$z = \mu + \sigma \cdot \varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$

The randomness $\varepsilon$ is now an input to the computation, not embedded in it. Gradients flow cleanly through $\mu$ and $\sigma$. You saw exactly this trick in Lecture 2 when we separated the noise from the diffusion forward process. Same idea, different context.

BatchNorm vs. GroupNorm: A Practical Note

Implementation Detail

VAE encoders and decoders typically use BatchNorm—this is one of the few places in the diffusion pipeline where BatchNorm appears. Our diffusion U-Net uses GroupNorm instead, because GroupNorm computes statistics per-sample (consistent behavior regardless of batch size), while BatchNorm statistics depend on the batch.

Why does this matter? During training, batch sizes are fixed and BatchNorm works fine. But during sampling, you might generate one image at a time (batch size 1), and BatchNorm's statistics become unreliable. GroupNorm doesn't have this problem.

The VAE sidesteps the issue entirely: it's trained separately and always runs in eval mode during diffusion training and sampling. In eval mode, BatchNorm uses frozen running statistics, so the batch size doesn't matter. Don't confuse the two: the VAE can use BatchNorm safely because it never trains jointly with the diffusion model.

The Latent Space

What does the latent space actually look like? Because of the KL regularization pushing the distribution toward $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$, the latent space has useful structure:

  • Nearby points in latent space map to similar images after decoding
  • Interpolation between two latent codes produces a smooth visual transition, not a jarring pixel-space average
  • The space is dense: most random samples from $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ decode to plausible-looking images (not noise or artifacts)

This last property is exactly why the VAE latent space is a good place to run diffusion. The diffusion model's job at the end of sampling is to produce a clean latent code $z_0$, and the VAE decoder can turn any reasonable latent code into a good image.

10.3 Latent Diffusion — The Key Insight

The key idea (Rombach et al., 2022): instead of running diffusion in pixel space, run it in the VAE's latent space. The recipe has three steps:

The Latent Diffusion Recipe
  1. Train a VAE on images—learn encoder $E$ and decoder $D$. Then freeze it completely.
  2. Encode all training images: $z_0 = E(x)$—you now have a dataset of latent codes instead of pixel images.
  3. Train a diffusion model on $z_0$ instead of $x$. The U-Net is smaller because it operates on $64 \times 64 \times 4$ latents instead of $512 \times 512 \times 3$ pixels.

At inference, the process runs in reverse:

  1. Sample $z_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ in latent space—shape $(1, 4, 64, 64)$
  2. Denoise: $z_T \to z_{T-1} \to \cdots \to z_0$ using the diffusion model (same DDPM/DDIM sampling we learned in Lectures 3 and 7)
  3. Decode: $x = D(z_0)$—back to pixel space, shape $(1, 3, 512, 512)$

Why This Works So Well

The two stages specialize in different things:

  • The VAE handles perceptual compression—it strips away imperceptible high-frequency detail that humans don't notice. The pixel-level reconstruction doesn't need to be perfect; it just needs to look right.
  • The diffusion model handles semantic generation—it only needs to model the meaningful structure of images (shapes, textures, compositions), not every individual pixel.

The compression from $512 \times 512 \times 3$ to $64 \times 64 \times 4$ is a 48× reduction in the number of values. The U-Net is proportionally smaller, attention is affordable (operating on $64 \times 64 = 4{,}096$ tokens instead of $512 \times 512 = 262{,}144$), and training is 10–100× cheaper than pixel-space diffusion at the same resolution.

Pseudocode

# Stage 1: Train VAE (done once, then frozen) vae = train_vae(image_dataset) # Stage 2: Encode all training images into latent space latent_dataset = [vae.encode(x) for x in image_dataset] # Stage 3: Train diffusion model on latents # Exactly the same training loop as Lecture 6, but on z_0 instead of x diffusion_model = train_diffusion(latent_dataset) # Inference: generate new images z_T = torch.randn(batch_size, 4, 64, 64) # start from noise in latent space z_0 = ddim_sample(diffusion_model, z_T, schedule, num_steps=50) images = vae.decode(z_0) # decode back to pixel space: (B, 3, 512, 512)

Notice how little changes from the MNIST pipeline in Lectures 3–7. The forward process, training loop, and sampling algorithm are identical; we've just swapped the data from pixels to latents.

10.4 The Stable Diffusion Architecture

Stable Diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022) is latent diffusion plus text conditioning. The architecture has three main components, each trained separately:

Component 1: The VAE

A KL-regularized autoencoder that handles the pixel↔latent conversion:

  • Encoder: $512 \times 512 \times 3 \to 64 \times 64 \times 4$ (8× spatial compression, ~48× total)
  • Decoder: $64 \times 64 \times 4 \to 512 \times 512 \times 3$
  • Trained separately on large image datasets, then frozen

Component 2: The U-Net

The diffusion model operates entirely in the $64 \times 64$ latent space. It's the same U-Net architecture we built in Lecture 4, with one addition: cross-attention layers that let the model attend to text embeddings. At each attention block, the image features are queries, and the text embeddings are keys and values. This is how the model "listens" to the text prompt.

The U-Net is time-conditioned (sinusoidal embeddings, just like Lectures 4–5) and much smaller than a pixel-space model at the same output resolution would be.

Component 3: The Text Encoder

Converts a text prompt into a sequence of embeddings that the U-Net can attend to:

  • SD 1.5: CLIP ViT-L/14—77 tokens, 768-dimensional embeddings
  • SDXL: dual text encoders (CLIP + OpenCLIP) for richer representations
  • SD 3: T5-XXL for more powerful text understanding

The text encoder is pretrained (on image-text pairs for CLIP, on text for T5) and frozen during diffusion training. Only the U-Net's weights are trained.

The Full Inference Pipeline

Stable Diffusion Inference
  1. Text encoding: prompt → text encoder → text embeddings with shape (seq_len, d_text)
  2. Sample noise: $z_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ with shape (1, 4, 64, 64)
  3. Iterative denoising (e.g., 50 DDIM steps):
    • U-Net predicts noise, conditioned on timestep $t$ and text embeddings (via cross-attention)
    • Classifier-free guidance (Lecture 9) combines conditional and unconditional predictions
  4. Decode: $x = D(z_0)$ → shape (1, 3, 512, 512)
  5. Denormalize from $[-1, 1]$ to $[0, 255]$ and display

Every component we've studied in this course shows up: the U-Net (Lecture 4), noise schedules (Lecture 5), the training loop (Lecture 6), DDIM sampling (Lecture 7), and classifier-free guidance (Lecture 9). Latent diffusion ties them all together into a system that actually works at scale.

10.5 The Evolution of Stable Diffusion

Stable Diffusion has evolved rapidly since its release, with each version improving text encoders, model capacity, and the underlying framework:

  • SD 1.5 — the original release. CLIP text encoder, $64 \times 64$ latent, ~860M U-Net parameters. $\varepsilon$-prediction with a linear noise schedule. The model that made open-source image generation mainstream.
  • SD 2.x — switched to OpenCLIP text encoder, adopted v-prediction (we'll cover this in Lecture 11), larger model. Better image quality, especially at higher resolutions.
  • SDXL — dual text encoders for richer text understanding, $128 \times 128$ base latent for higher fidelity, plus a separate refiner model. Significantly better prompt adherence.
  • SD 3 — a major architecture shift: replaces the U-Net with a Diffusion Transformer (Lecture 12), uses T5-XXL text encoder, and adopts flow matching (Lecture 13) instead of DDPM. The state of the art.
Key Insight
The trend across SD versions is clear: better text encoders, larger models, and more advanced training frameworks. But the core latent diffusion insight—compress with a VAE, diffuse in latent space—remains unchanged. It's the foundation that everything else builds on.

10.6 Looking Ahead
