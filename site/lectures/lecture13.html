Flow Matching & Rectified Flows

Dropping the noise schedule, the ELBO, and the cumulative alpha products. Learning straight-line velocity fields from noise to data.

What if we could generate images from noise with dramatically less scaffolding? In this lecture, we trace the arc from DDPM to a cleaner modern framework called flow matching. We catalogue DDPM's machinery, then show how flow matching replaces all of it with straight-line transport and learned velocity fields.

This Lecture Is For You If
  • You want to understand flow matching, a simpler alternative to DDPM
  • You're comfortable with ODEs and vector fields, or willing to build intuition

13.1 DDPM's Baggage — What We're Trying to Escape

Let's take a step back and appreciate just how much intricate machinery DDPM requires. We've built all of it through this course, piece by piece, and each part made sense on its own. But look at the massive, full stack of interlocking pieces:

  • A noise schedule — choose βt\beta_tβt​ values (linear? cosine? sigmoid?), then compute cumulative products αˉt=∏s=1t(1−βs)\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t}(1 - \beta_s)αˉt​=∏s=1t​(1−βs​)
  • A zoo of precomputed tensors — αˉt\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}αˉt​​, 1−αˉt\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}1−αˉt​​, posterior variance β~t\tilde{\beta}_tβ~​t​, and more. We built a whole prepare_schedule() function just to manage them.
  • A long derivation chain — forward process → Bayes' rule for the posterior → variational lower bound (ELBO) → KL between Gaussians → noise reparameterization → simplified loss
  • 1000 discrete timesteps for full-quality sampling (or DDIM tricks to reduce it)
  • Schedule sensitivity — the choice between linear, cosine, and sigmoid schedules meaningfully affects results. The schedule is a sensitive hyperparameter.

All of this works, as we've seen throughout this course. But it's a lot of machinery for what should be a simple goal: transport samples from N(0,I)\mathcal{N}(0, I)N(0,I) to the data distribution.

13.2 The Bridge: v-Prediction to Flow Matching

The path from DDPM to flow matching ran through an intermediate improvement called v-prediction (Salimans & Ho, 2022). Understanding that middle step shows how flow matching follows naturally from earlier work.

The Problem with Noise Prediction

As we saw in Lecture 11, DDPM's standard ϵ\epsilonϵ-prediction target has uneven difficulty across timesteps: too little signal at the boundaries, unstable gradients at the extremes. v-prediction fixed the target, but not the framework around it.

v-Prediction: The Right Idea, the Wrong Framework

Salimans & Ho (2022) proposed v-prediction to fix this. Instead of predicting the noise ϵ\epsilonϵ, predict a velocity:

v=αˉt⋅ϵ−1−αˉt⋅x0v = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \cdot \epsilon - \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \cdot x_0v=αˉt​​⋅ϵ−1−αˉt​​⋅x0​

This was a real improvement. The prediction target balances the difficulty across all timesteps, with no more extreme behavior at the boundaries. Imagen and Stable Diffusion 2.x adopted v-prediction for exactly this reason.

But here's what v-prediction didn't fix:

  • You still need a noise schedule (βt\beta_tβt​ values)
  • You still need cumulative products (αˉt\bar{\alpha}_tαˉt​)
  • You still need the full ELBO-derived loss structure
  • You still sample in 50–1000 discrete steps through the schedule
  • The entire forward/reverse process machinery stays intact

v-prediction fixed the prediction target, but it didn't simplify the framework.

The concept was right (think in terms of velocities), but it was still trapped inside DDPM's schedule-driven noising process.

Key Insight
v-prediction showed that thinking in terms of velocities was more natural than predicting noise. But the DDPM framework constrained what "velocity" could mean — it was a specific linear combination of x0x_0x0​ and ϵ\epsilonϵ, tied to the noise schedule's αˉt\bar{\alpha}_tαˉt​ values. What if we designed a framework where velocity was the fundamental concept, not a derived one?

Reframing Generation as Particle Transport

Setting aside the DDPM framework, consider the problem from first principles.

We have two distributions: a data distribution pdatap_{\text{data}}pdata​ (images of digits, faces, whatever) and a noise distribution N(0,I)\mathcal{N}(0, I)N(0,I) (easy to sample from). We want to learn a mapping from one to the other.

Imagine each sample as a particle. Generation means moving particles from their starting positions (noise) to their destinations (data). Each particle follows a path through space, and at every point along that path, it has a velocity, a direction and speed.

A velocity field v(x,t)v(x, t)v(x,t) tells each particle which direction to move at each moment in time. If we know the velocity field, generating a sample is just solving an ordinary differential equation:

dxdt=vθ(xt,t)\frac{dx}{dt} = v_\theta(x_t, t)dtdx​=vθ​(xt​,t)

Start at a noise sample, follow the velocity field, arrive at a data sample.

What This Framework Needs

To make this concrete, we need just three things:

  1. A path connecting each noise sample to each data sample (the interpolation)
  2. A target velocity along that path (what the model learns to predict)
  3. An ODE solver to follow the learned velocity field at inference

That's the complete list: no schedule, no ELBO, no reverse process derivation, no cumulative alpha products.

The question is: what paths should we use?

13.3 Straight-Line Interpolation

Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023) makes the simplest possible choice: straight lines.

Define the interpolation path between data x0x_0x0​ and noise x1x_1x1​:

xt=(1−t)⋅x0+t⋅x1t∈[0,1]x_t = (1 - t) \cdot x_0 + t \cdot x_1 \qquad t \in [0, 1]xt​=(1−t)⋅x0​+t⋅x1​t∈[0,1]

Let's verify this does what we want:

  • At t=0t = 0t=0: xt=x0x_t = x_0xt​=x0​ (pure data)
  • At t=1t = 1t=1: xt=x1x_t = x_1xt​=x1​ (pure noise)
  • In between: a linear blend of data and noise

The velocity along this straight path is trivially computed:

dxdt=x1−x0\frac{dx}{dt} = x_1 - x_0dtdx​=x1​−x0​

That's a constant velocity; it doesn't depend on ttt at all. Each particle just moves in a straight line from data to noise at uniform speed.

⚠ Convention Warning

In this lecture, we use t=0t = 0t=0 for data and t=1t = 1t=1 for noise. Some papers use the opposite convention. Always check! The original DDPM convention has t=0t = 0t=0 as clean and t=Tt = Tt=T as noise, which is consistent with what we use here (if you think of t=1t = 1t=1 as the "end"). But other flow matching papers flip this, with t=0t = 0t=0 as noise and t=1t = 1t=1 as data.

13.4 The Flow Matching Training Objective

Flow matching's simplicity shows in the training algorithm. The entire training algorithm is six lines:

Flow Matching Training
  1. Sample data: x0∼pdatax_0 \sim p_{\text{data}}x0​∼pdata​
  2. Sample noise: x1∼N(0,I)x_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)x1​∼N(0,I)
  3. Sample time: t∼Uniform(0,1)t \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)t∼Uniform(0,1)
  4. Interpolate: xt=(1−t)⋅x0+t⋅x1x_t = (1 - t) \cdot x_0 + t \cdot x_1xt​=(1−t)⋅x0​+t⋅x1​
  5. Target velocity: u=x1−x0u = x_1 - x_0u=x1​−x0​
  6. Loss: L=∥vθ(xt,t)−u∥2\mathcal{L} = \| v_\theta(x_t, t) - u \|^2L=∥vθ​(xt​,t)−u∥2

The training loop requires no cumulative alpha products, no schedule tensors, and no reparameterization trick. You interpolate between data and noise along a straight line, and train the model to predict the velocity of that line.

Pseudocode: Flow Matching Training Step

def flow_matching_train_step(model, x_0): x_1 = randn_like(x_0) # sample noise t = rand(batch_size) # t ~ Uniform(0, 1) x_t = (1 - t) * x_0 + t * x_1 # straight-line interpolation target_velocity = x_1 - x_0 # constant velocity along the path velocity_pred = model(x_t, t) # neural network predicts velocity loss = mse_loss(velocity_pred, target_velocity) return loss

Side-by-Side: DDPM vs. Flow Matching

The complexity difference is visible in the code. Here's the interpolation step for each:

# DDPM: schedule-dependent, needs precomputed tensors alphas_cumprod = cumprod(1 - betas) # schedule computation sqrt_alpha_bar = sqrt(alphas_cumprod[t]) # schedule indexing sqrt_one_minus = sqrt(1 - alphas_cumprod[t]) # more schedule indexing x_t = sqrt_alpha_bar * x_0 + sqrt_one_minus * noise # weighted combination # Flow matching: schedule-free, just linear interpolation x_t = (1 - t) * x_0 + t * noise # linear interpolation

DDPM's interpolation coefficients (αˉt\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}αˉt​​ and 1−αˉt\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}1−αˉt​​) are nonlinear functions of the noise schedule, precomputed and stored in dictionaries. Flow matching's coefficients are (1−t)(1 - t)(1−t) and ttt, literally just the time variable.

Wait, Why Didn't We Just Do This From the Start?

If you're reading this and thinking "that's it? Linear interpolation and an MSE loss?" — you're not wrong. Flow matching is remarkably simple compared to the DDPM machinery we spent lectures building up. But that simplicity is the result of years of theoretical work, not a sign that the earlier approaches were wrong. DDPM established that iterative denoising could generate high-quality images. Score matching connected diffusion to SDEs and continuous-time theory. v-prediction showed that thinking in terms of velocities stabilized training. Each step made the next simplification possible. Flow matching is obvious in hindsight, which is the hallmark of a good idea — but it took the entire arc to see it clearly.

13.5 Sampling via ODE Integration

To generate images, we start from pure noise and follow the learned velocity field backward in time. This is an ordinary differential equation (ODE), and we solve it with numerical integration.

Euler Method

The simplest approach: take fixed-size steps along the velocity field.

def flow_matching_sample(model, shape, num_steps=50): x = randn(shape) # x_1 ~ N(0, I) dt = 1.0 / num_steps for i in range(num_steps): t = 1.0 - i * dt # t goes from 1 -> 0 velocity = model(x, t) x = x - dt * velocity # Euler step return x # x_0 (generated data)

At each step, we ask the model "given this current state xxx at time ttt, what's the velocity?" and take a small step in that direction. After enough steps, we arrive at data.

Better ODE Solvers

Euler's method works but is first-order; errors accumulate, and you need many steps for accuracy. Better options include:

  • Heun's method (2nd-order) — takes a trial step, evaluates the velocity at the new position, then averages. Roughly halves the steps needed for the same quality.
  • RK4 (4th-order Runge-Kutta) — the classic ODE solver. Four velocity evaluations per step, but far more accurate.
  • Adaptive step-size solvers — automatically choose step sizes based on local error estimates. More function evaluations where the flow is complex, fewer where it's simple.

In practice, 20–50 Euler steps produce good results for flow matching. Compare this to DDPM's 1000 steps — or even DDIM's 50–100.

13.6 Why Flow Matching Works Better

Flow matching has genuine advantages over DDPM beyond implementation simplicity:

No Schedule to Design

There are no βt\beta_tβt​ values, no αt\alpha_tαt​, no αˉt\bar{\alpha}_tαˉt​. The interpolation path is the "schedule," and it's always a straight line. One less hyperparameter to tune, one less thing to get wrong.

Simpler Math

No ELBO derivation, no KL divergence between Gaussians, no reparameterization trick. The loss is direct: predict the velocity, minimize MSE. The entire framework can be derived in a few pages of algebra.

More Stable Training

The target velocity (x1−x0)(x_1 - x_0)(x1​−x0​) has roughly constant magnitude regardless of ttt. Compare this to ϵ\epsilonϵ-prediction, where the signal-to-noise ratio changes dramatically across timesteps. Constant-magnitude targets mean the loss landscape is smoother and gradients are more uniform.

Straight Paths Need Fewer Steps

Because the learned flow approximately follows straight lines, ODE solvers can take larger steps without losing accuracy. Curved paths (like DDPM's) require small steps to stay on track. Straight paths can be traversed accurately with larger step sizes.

13.7 Rectified Flows

There's a subtlety we've been glossing over. Even though each individual training pair (x0,x1)(x_0, x_1)(x0​,x1​) defines a straight path, the aggregate learned flow isn't perfectly straight, because different data-noise pairs share regions of space, and their flows interact. Imagine two straight arrows crossing through the same point — the average velocity at that point is a compromise between the two directions, creating curvature.

The Rectification Idea

Liu et al. (2023) proposed rectified flows to fix this. The idea is to iteratively straighten the learned trajectories:

Rectified Flow Training
  1. Train an initial flow matching model vθv_\thetavθ​
  2. Generate coupled pairs (x0,x1)(x_0, x_1)(x0​,x1​) by running the model forward and backward — these are matched noise-data pairs, not independent samples
  3. Retrain on these coupled pairs — because the pairs are now correlated (each noise sample is matched to the data sample it would generate), the new flow is straighter
  4. Repeat: use the retrained model as the new initial model, go back to step 2 to generate even better linked pairs, and retrain again. Each round straightens the flow further. In practice, 1–2 rounds of rectification is enough — diminishing returns set in quickly.

The key insight: in the first round, each data point x0x_0x0​ is paired with a random noise sample x1x_1x1​ — there's no correspondence between them. A cat image gets paired with some arbitrary blob of noise, and a dog image gets paired with a completely different blob. The straight lines between these random pairs inevitably cross each other, forcing the model to learn curved paths to avoid collisions.

Random pairing → paths must curve to avoid crossing

After training the first model, we use it to generate data: start from a noise sample, run the ODE solver, and arrive at a generated image. Now we have something new: a noise sample and the image it actually produced, connected by the path the model learned. These are linked pairs. The noise and data are no longer randomly matched — this particular noise blob led to this particular cat image through the flow.

Linked pairing → straight paths, no crossings

When we retrain on these linked pairs, the straight lines between them are much less tangled. The model already sorted out which noise goes to which output in the first round, so the second round of straight-line targets has far fewer crossings. The result is a flow field that is closer to straight, meaning the ODE solver can follow it accurately in fewer steps.

The Payoff: Fewer Steps

Straighter trajectories mean the ODE solver can take larger steps without deviating from the true path. The practical impact is dramatic:

  • Without rectification: 20–50 steps for good quality
  • With 1–2 rounds of rectification: as few as 1–4 steps for comparable quality

This is used in Stable Diffusion 3 and InstaFlow for fast, high-quality generation.

13.8 Connection to Diffusion

Flow matching is a continuous generalization of diffusion. Let's make the connection explicit.

The Interpolation Path Is the Difference

Both DDPM and flow matching define a path from data to noise. The paths are different:

# DDPM path (curved, schedule-dependent) x_t = √ᾱ_t · x_0 + √(1 - ᾱ_t) · ε # discrete t ∈ {0, ..., T-1} # Flow matching path (straight, schedule-free) x_t = (1 - t) · x_0 + t · x_1 # continuous t ∈ [0, 1]

DDPM's coefficients αˉt\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}αˉt​​ and 1−αˉt\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}1−αˉt​​ are nonlinear functions of the schedule — they trace a curved path through the space between data and noise. Flow matching's coefficients (1−t)(1 - t)(1−t) and ttt trace a straight line.

Time Conventions

The time mapping is also different:

  • DDPM: discrete timesteps t∈{0,1,…,T−1}t \in \{0, 1, \ldots, T-1\}t∈{0,1,…,T−1}, typically T=1000T = 1000T=1000
  • Flow matching: continuous time t∈[0,1]t \in [0, 1]t∈[0,1]

Roughly, DDPM's discrete timestep tddpmt_{\text{ddpm}}tddpm​ corresponds to flow matching's continuous tfm≈tddpm/Tt_{\text{fm}} \approx t_{\text{ddpm}} / Ttfm​≈tddpm​/T. But the interpolation coefficients are fundamentally different — DDPM uses schedule-derived nonlinear weights, while flow matching uses linear weights.

The Unified Picture

Diffusion, score matching, and flow matching are all ways to learn a mapping from noise to data. They differ in three dimensions:

DDPM Score Matching Flow Matching
Interpolation path Curved (schedule-dependent) Curved (SDE drift) Straight (linear)
Training target ϵ\epsilonϵ (noise) ∇xlog⁡pt(x)\nabla_x \log p_t(x)∇x​logpt​(x) (score) vvv (velocity)
Sampling Discrete SDE/DDIM SDE or probability flow ODE ODE (Euler, Heun, RK4)

The deep connection: DDPM can be viewed as flow matching with a specific non-straight interpolation path. The probability flow ODE from Lecture 7 is the bridge — it showed that DDPM's discrete sampling process has a continuous ODE equivalent. Flow matching applies this insight by using a simpler ODE from the start.

Key Insight
The progression from DDPM to flow matching is a simplification. DDPM proved that the "noise to data" idea works. Score matching showed it has a continuous formulation. v-prediction showed that velocity is a better prediction target. Flow matching completed the arc: build the entire framework around velocity and straight-line transport, dropping everything that was incidental to the core idea.

13.9 Practical Impact

Flow matching with rectified flows is now the default framework for frontier generative models:

  • Stable Diffusion 3 (Stability AI) — uses flow matching with rectified flows, not DDPM. Combined with the MM-DiT architecture from Lecture 12.
  • Flux (Black Forest Labs) — flow matching + DiT, from the original Stable Diffusion creators.
  • Training recipe — simpler than DDPM. Fewer hyperparameters (no schedule choice), more robust to configuration changes.
  • Sampling — 20–50 Euler steps for good quality. With rectification, as few as 4 steps.

The trend is clear: flow matching is replacing DDPM as the default framework, offering simpler math, fewer hyperparameters, and better scaling.

We experimented with noise schedules and then threw them away entirely. Each step simplified the framework, removing complexity that was not essential to the core idea of transporting noise into data.
