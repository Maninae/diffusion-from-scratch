Why Diffusion? The Generative Models Landscape

Every generative framework makes a tradeoff. Diffusion models found a way to avoid the worst of them—by learning to reverse destruction instead of creating from scratch.

Foundations Generative Models Motivation

How do you teach a machine to create images that never existed?

Every major generative framework — GANs, VAEs, normalizing flows, autoregressive models — found a different answer, and each one paid a different price. We'll survey those tradeoffs to understand the gap that diffusion models fill: stable training, full mode coverage, and high-quality samples, all from a single, elegant idea — learning to reverse destruction.

The Generative Modeling Problem

Here's the setup. You have a dataset of images—say, a million photographs of faces. These images were drawn from some true distribution $p_{\text{data}}(x)$ that we can never write down in closed form.

We want to do two things: (1) learn a model that captures this distribution, and (2) sample new images from it that look like they belong in the original dataset.

This is the generative modeling problem, and it's deceptively hard.

Images live in extremely high-dimensional spaces—a 256×256 RGB image has 196,608 pixel values. The distribution over these values is incredibly complex: most random configurations of 196K numbers look like static, not faces. The "face manifold" is a thin, winding surface in a vast space, and we need to find it from examples alone.

When evaluating a generative model, we care about several things:

  • Sample quality — Do generated images look realistic? Are they sharp and diverse?
  • Training stability — Can we reliably train the model without it collapsing or diverging?
  • Mode coverage — Does the model capture the full diversity of the data, or does it only produce a few types of output?
  • Likelihood — Can the model tell us how probable a given image is?

No framework gets all of these for free. Every approach makes a tradeoff—and understanding those tradeoffs is the key to understanding why diffusion models exist.

The Generative Models Zoo

Before diffusion models, four major frameworks dominated generative modeling. Each one found a clever way to learn $p(x)$—and each one paid a price.

GANs — Generative Adversarial Networks

GANs (Goodfellow et al., 2014) frame generation as a game between two networks. A generator $G$ tries to produce realistic images from random noise. A discriminator $D$ tries to distinguish real images from generated ones. They train against each other: $G$ improves at fooling $D$, and $D$ improves at catching $G$.

GANs produce stunningly sharp images—for years, they held the crown for visual quality.

But the adversarial training dynamic is fragile. Generators frequently suffer from mode collapse, where they learn to produce only a few types of output that fool the discriminator. Training requires careful hyperparameter tuning, and there's no reliable way to measure convergence. You also get no density estimation—GANs can't tell you how likely a given image is.

VAEs — Variational Autoencoders

VAEs (Kingma & Welling, 2014) take a probabilistic approach. An encoder maps images to a low-dimensional latent space, and a decoder reconstructs images from latent codes. The model is trained by maximizing a lower bound on the log-likelihood (the ELBO).

VAEs give you a proper probabilistic framework—smooth latent spaces, meaningful interpolations, and a principled training objective. But VAE samples tend to be blurry. The reconstruction loss (typically MSE) encourages the decoder to hedge its bets, producing the average of possible outputs rather than committing to sharp details.

Normalizing Flows

Normalizing flows (Rezende & Mohamed, 2015) build a chain of invertible transformations that map a simple distribution (Gaussian) to the data distribution. Because every transformation is invertible, you can compute exact log-likelihoods using the change-of-variables formula.

This is mathematically elegant—exact density evaluation is a powerful tool. But the invertibility constraint severely limits what architectures you can use. You can't use standard convolutions, attention, or most modern building blocks. This architectural straitjacket limits the expressiveness of the model.

Autoregressive Models

Autoregressive models (van den Oord et al., 2016) factor the joint distribution as a product of conditionals: $p(x) = \prod_i p(x_i \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{i-1})$. They predict one pixel (or token) at a time, conditioned on everything generated so far.

This gives you exact likelihoods and powerful density estimation—PixelCNN and its variants are excellent at capturing fine-grained dependencies. The main limitation is that generation is sequential. To produce a 256×256 image, you need 65,536 sequential forward passes. You can't parallelize this. For large images, generation is painfully slow.

Key Insight

Notice the pattern: every framework trades something essential. GANs sacrifice stability and mode coverage. VAEs sacrifice sharpness. Flows sacrifice architectural freedom. Autoregressive models sacrifice generation speed. Is there a framework that avoids the worst of these tradeoffs?

Enter Diffusion Models

Diffusion models take a completely different angle. Instead of trying to learn the data distribution in one shot—through a generator, a decoder, an invertible transform, or a sequential factorization—they learn to gradually reverse a simple destruction process.

The core intuition is simple:

Core Idea

Destroying an image is easy—just add noise. If we add Gaussian noise step by step, any image eventually becomes pure static. What if we could train a neural network to undo each step of that destruction?

This gives us two processes:

  • The forward process — Gradually add Gaussian noise over $T$ steps (typically $T = 1000$). A clean image $x_0$ becomes progressively noisier: $x_0 \to x_1 \to x_2 \to \cdots \to x_T$. By step $T$, the image is indistinguishable from pure random noise.
  • The reverse process — Train a neural network to predict and remove the noise at each step. Starting from pure noise $x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, the network iteratively denoises: $x_T \to x_{T-1} \to \cdots \to x_0$. The result is a clean, generated image.

Why does this work so well? Because each denoising step is a small, learnable problem. The network doesn't have to generate an entire image from scratch—it just has to remove a tiny bit of noise at each step. A thousand easy problems instead of one impossible one.

Diffusion models deliver on these tradeoffs:

  • Stable training — The loss is a simple MSE (predict the noise that was added). No adversarial dynamics, no mode collapse.
  • State-of-the-art sample quality — Diffusion models match or exceed GANs on FID scores while maintaining full mode coverage.
  • Flexible architecture — No invertibility constraints. You can use any architecture: U-Nets, transformers, attention—whatever works best.
  • Principled framework — Built on a variational bound with a clean probabilistic interpretation.

The main cost is sampling speed. Generating an image requires iterating through hundreds or thousands of denoising steps. This is the main weakness, and much of the research since 2020 has focused on reducing the number of steps needed. We'll cover accelerated sampling in later lectures.

Historical Timeline

Diffusion models didn't appear overnight. The journey from theoretical curiosity to the backbone of modern generative AI spans nearly a decade.

2015 — The Original Idea

Sohl-Dickstein et al. published "Deep Unsupervised Learning using Nonequilibrium Thermodynamics"—the paper that started it all. They drew on ideas from statistical physics: a diffusion process that gradually destroys data, paired with a learned reverse process. The framework was theoretically elegant but produced modest results.

It wasn't competitive with GANs, and the community largely moved on.

2020 — The DDPM Breakthrough

Five years later, Ho, Jain, and Abbeel published "Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models" (DDPM). They stripped the framework to its essentials: a simple noise schedule, a U-Net architecture, and an MSE loss on predicted noise. The result was competitive image quality with remarkably stable training.

Two algorithms—Algorithm 1 (training) and Algorithm 2 (sampling)—became the foundation of everything in this course.

2021 — Beating GANs

Dhariwal and Nichol at OpenAI showed that diffusion models could surpass GANs on ImageNet FID—a watershed moment. Their key contributions: classifier guidance (steering generation with a classifier's gradients) and improved architecture choices. In the same year, Y. Song et al. unified diffusion models and score matching into a continuous-time SDE framework.

2022–Present — The Diffusion Explosion

Rombach et al. introduced Latent Diffusion—running the diffusion process in a compressed latent space instead of pixel space. This made high-resolution generation practical and became Stable Diffusion, one of the most widely used generative models in history. Since then: DALL-E 2, Imagen, SDXL, Sora, Flux. Diffusion is now the default backbone for image, video, and audio generation.

Perspective

From a niche physics-inspired paper in 2015 to the engine behind Stable Diffusion and Sora in under a decade. The core algorithm is nearly identical—what changed was the engineering: better architectures, better schedules, better conditioning, and the latent space trick. Understanding the fundamentals gives you access to all of it.
